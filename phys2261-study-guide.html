Chapter 1

Systems, the Zeroth Law and Temperature

What this chapter does

Thermodynamics describes huge numbers of particles with a handful of numbers (\(P\), \(V\), \(T\), \(U\), …). This chapter sets up the vocabulary for that: what a "system" is, what kinds of changes it can undergo, which quantities describe it, and, most importantly, why temperature is a well-defined thing you can measure at all.

After this chapter you should be able to
  • classify systems (isolated / closed / open) and processes (spontaneous / quasi-static / reversible);
  • tell extensive from intensive variables;
  • explain what the Zeroth Law guarantees and how the Kelvin and ideal-gas temperature scales are built.

1.1  Systems and their walls

A thermodynamic system is whatever piece of the world we choose to study: a gas in a cylinder, a cup of coffee, a star. Everything else is the surroundings. The boundary between them is called a wall, and what matters about a wall is what it lets through.

SystemEnergy crosses?Matter crosses?Example
IsolatedNoNoGas in a rigid, perfectly insulated box
ClosedYes (heat or work)NoGas in a cylinder with a movable piston
OpenYesYesA pot of boiling water (steam escapes)
Where it's used later

Chapters 1–5 mostly treat closed systems. Isolated systems are where the Second Law is cleanest ("entropy never decreases", Chapter 4). Open systems need the chemical potential (Chapter 5) and are the setting for phase transitions and solutions (Chapters 7–8).

1.2  Equilibrium and kinds of processes

A system is in (thermal) equilibrium when its macroscopic properties (pressure, temperature, density, …) stay constant in time and nothing flows inside it or across its walls. Only then are quantities like "the pressure" or "the temperature" of the system well defined. A process takes the system from one equilibrium state to another, and processes come in three kinds:

Spontaneous
Happens by itself once a constraint is removed, e.g. gas rushing into a vacuum when a partition breaks. In between, the system is out of equilibrium, so \(P\) and \(T\) may not even be defined.
Quasi-static
Carried out so slowly that the system is essentially in equilibrium at every step. Only these can be drawn as curves on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram.
Reversible
Quasi-static and with no dissipation (no friction, no heat flowing across a finite temperature gap). It can be run backwards so that the system and its surroundings both return exactly to where they started. An idealisation, like a frictionless pulley.
Why the distinction matters

The formula for work \(W = -\int P\,dV\) (Chapter 3) only applies to quasi-static processes, because otherwise \(P\) isn't defined. The formula for entropy \(\Delta S = \int \text{đ}Q/T\) (Chapter 4) only applies along reversible paths. Many exam questions turn on noticing that a process is not quasi-static (sudden expansion, a released piston, a weight dropped on a piston).

1.3  State variables: extensive and intensive

State variables describe an equilibrium state. A quick test sorts them into two kinds: cut the system in half and see what happens to the variable.

KindWhen you halve the systemExamples
Extensivehalves\(V\), \(U\), \(N\), mass, \(S\)
Intensivestays the same\(T\), \(P\), density, \(\mu\)

A minimal set of variables that fixes the equilibrium state completely is called the set of thermodynamic coordinates. All other state variables are functions of them. The coordinates are taken to be extensive: the internal energy \(U\) plus "work coordinates" such as \(V\) (or a magnetisation \(M\) for a magnet, an area for a film).

Why not just use \(T\) and \(P\)? Take three containers of water at 0 °C and 1 atm: all ice, half ice and half water, all water. Same \(T\), same \(P\), but clearly different states with different internal energies. \(T\) and \(P\) can't tell them apart; an extensive variable like \(U\) can.

1.4  The Zeroth Law: why temperature exists

The Zeroth Law

If A is in thermal equilibrium with C, and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A is in thermal equilibrium with B.

It sounds obvious, but it is exactly what makes a thermometer work. C is the thermometer: if it reads the same in A and in B, we can conclude that A and B would be in equilibrium with each other without ever putting them in contact. Mathematically, the Zeroth Law guarantees that every system has a function of its own coordinates, \(\Theta\), that takes the same value for all systems in mutual equilibrium:

Consequence

\[ \Theta_A(A_1, A_2, \ldots) = \Theta_B(B_1, B_2, \ldots) \quad\text{whenever A and B are in equilibrium} \]

That function is the temperature. The Zeroth Law tells us it exists; it does not yet tell us what numbers to assign. That is the job of a temperature scale.

Proof: from the Zeroth Law to a temperature function

Equilibrium between A and C imposes one condition linking their coordinates, \(f_{AC}(A_1, A_2, \ldots; C_1, C_2, \ldots) = 0\); similarly \(f_{BC}(B_1, \ldots; C_1, \ldots) = 0\). Solve both for \(C_1\):

\[ C_1 = F_{AC}(A_1, A_2, \ldots; C_2, \ldots) = F_{BC}(B_1, B_2, \ldots; C_2, \ldots) \]

The Zeroth Law says A and B are then also in equilibrium, \(f_{AB}(A_1, \ldots; B_1, \ldots) = 0\), a condition with no C-coordinates in it. So the C-coordinates must cancel out of the equation above, leaving \(\Theta_A(A_1, A_2, \ldots) = \Theta_B(B_1, B_2, \ldots)\).

1.5  Temperature scales

To put numbers on temperature, pick a thermometric property \(X\) that changes with hotness (the length of a mercury column, the pressure of a gas at fixed volume, a wire's resistance) and assume a linear rule \(\Theta = aX + b\), with \(a\), \(b\) fixed by reference points.

Celsius, absolute zero, Kelvin

Celsius uses two reference points, ice melting (0 °C) and water boiling at 1 atm (100 °C), and interpolates linearly between them. Now measure the volume of a dilute gas at fixed pressure at several temperatures and extrapolate the straight line to \(V = 0\). Whatever the gas and whatever the pressure, the line hits zero at \(-273.15\ \text{°C}\). Since a volume can't be negative, nothing can be colder: that is absolute zero. The Kelvin scale keeps the Celsius degree size but starts there:

Conversions

\[ T\ (\text{K}) = \theta\ (\text{°C}) + 273.15, \qquad \theta\ (\text{°C}) = \tfrac{5}{9}\,(\theta_F - 32) \]

Always use kelvin in formulas. Ratios like \(T_2/T_1\), \(\ln(T_2/T_1)\) or \(1 - T_C/T_H\) are meaningless in Celsius. Temperature differences are the same in both.

The ideal-gas temperature scale

Different thermometers (different substances, different amounts of gas) disagree slightly with each other. But as a gas thermometer is made more and more dilute, all gases converge on the same reading. That limit defines a universal scale, anchored at the triple point of water (273.16 K, where ice, water and vapour coexist):

Ideal-gas temperature (constant-volume gas thermometer)

\[ T = 273.16\ \text{K}\times\lim_{P_{\text{tr}}\to0}\frac{P}{P_{\text{tr}}} \]

\(P_{\text{tr}}\) is the gas pressure when the bulb sits at the triple point; \(P\) is the pressure at the unknown temperature. Chapter 4 shows that this scale coincides with the "thermodynamic" temperature defined by heat engines, so it is not tied to any particular substance.

Example · Reading a gas thermometer

A dilute constant-volume gas thermometer reads \(P_{\text{tr}} = 50.0\ \text{kPa}\) at the triple point and \(68.3\ \text{kPa}\) in boiling water. What temperature does it give?

Show solution

\(T = 273.16 \times 68.3/50.0 \approx 373.1\ \text{K}\), i.e. about 100 °C. (In practice you repeat with less and less gas and extrapolate the ratio to \(P_{\text{tr}} \to 0\).)

Chapter 2

Gases, Kinetic Theory and Response Coefficients

What this chapter does

An equation of state ties \(P\), \(V\), \(T\) together for a given substance. This chapter collects the ones we use (ideal gas, van der Waals), then looks underneath: kinetic theory explains pressure and temperature in terms of molecules bouncing around, which gives us \(U = \tfrac{f}{2}nRT\), the source of every heat capacity in Chapter 3. Finally it introduces the measurable coefficients \(\alpha\), \(\kappa_T\), \(\beta\) and the partial-derivative tools you will use for the rest of the course.

After this chapter you should be able to
  • use \(PV = nRT\) and explain the corrections in the van der Waals equation;
  • derive \(PV = \tfrac{2}{3}K\), use equipartition to find \(U\) for monatomic and diatomic gases, and compute characteristic molecular speeds;
  • work with \(\alpha\), \(\kappa_T\), \(\beta\) and the cyclic relation.

2.1  The ideal gas law

An equation of state is a relation \(f(P, V, T) = 0\) among the state variables of a substance in equilibrium. Thermodynamics does not predict it; it comes from experiment or from a microscopic model. The simplest, and the limit every real gas approaches at low density, is

Ideal gas law

\[ PV = nRT = Nk_BT, \qquad R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}, \quad k_B = R/N_A = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K} \]

It is really the ideal-gas temperature scale of §1.5 rewritten: that scale is defined so that \(PV/n\) of a dilute gas is proportional to \(T\), and \(R\) is the proportionality constant. Useful forms: density \(\rho = PM/(RT)\) with \(M\) the molar mass; and \(1\ \text{atm·L} = 101.3\ \text{J}\).

Example 1 · Hot-air balloon (Tut 1)

A balloon of volume 1800 m³ carries 500 kg (envelope + payload). Outside air is at 290 K and 1 atm, with molar mass 29 g/mol; inside is at the same pressure. (i) What must the inside temperature be for the balloon to float? (ii) What is the mass of hot air inside?

Show solution

Outside density: \(\rho_{\text{out}} = PM/(RT) = (1.01\times10^5)(0.029)/(8.314\times290) \approx 1.215\ \text{kg/m}^3\), so the displaced air has mass \(1.215\times1800 \approx 2187\ \text{kg}\).

Floating: weight of displaced air = payload + hot air, so the hot air has mass \(2187 - 500 = 1687\ \text{kg}\) — that's (ii).

Same pressure inside and out means \(\rho T\) is the same: \(T_{\text{in}} = T_{\text{out}}\,\rho_{\text{out}}/\rho_{\text{in}} = 290 \times 2187/1687 \approx 376\ \text{K}\) (about 103 °C) — that's (i).

Example 2 · Pressure falls with height (barometric formula, Tut 1)

A thin slab of air at height \(z\) is held up by the pressure difference across it. Assuming constant \(T\), find \(P(z)\).

Show solution

Force balance on a slab of area \(A\), thickness \(dz\): \(P(z)A = P(z + dz)A + \rho g A\,dz\), so \(dP/dz = -\rho g = -\frac{Mg}{RT}P\). Separating and integrating:

\[ P(z) = P_0\,e^{-Mgz/RT} \]

The pressure drops by a factor \(e\) every \(RT/(Mg) = (8.314\times290)/(0.029\times9.8) \approx 8.5\ \text{km}\). (Chapter 3 redoes this with temperature falling with height.)

2.2  Real gases

Real molecules have a size and attract each other, so at high density (high pressure, low temperature) the ideal gas law fails. Two standard corrections:

Van der Waals equation

\[ \left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT \]

  • \(-nb\): molecules take up space. The volume available to move in is less than the container volume.
  • \(+an^2/V^2\): molecules attract. A molecule about to hit the wall is pulled back by its neighbours, so it hits more softly and the measured \(P\) is lower than it would otherwise be. The effect grows with the square of the density.
Virial expansion (a systematic series in density) \[ PV = nRT\left(1 + \frac{B(T)}{V/n} + \frac{C(T)}{(V/n)^2} + \cdots\right) \]

At low density every correction vanishes and \(PV = nRT\) returns.

Where it's used later

The van der Waals gas is the course's standard "non-ideal" example: its entropy (Chapter 4), why it cools in a free expansion (Chapter 5), and how it produces the liquid–gas transition and a critical point (Chapter 7).

2.3  Kinetic theory: pressure from molecules

Model the gas as many tiny hard spheres in random motion, colliding elastically with each other and the walls, with no forces between collisions and no preferred direction. Pressure is then just the momentum delivered to the walls per unit area per unit time. Working this out gives

Pressure from molecular motion

\[ PV = \tfrac{1}{3}Nm\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac{2}{3}K, \qquad K = N\cdot\tfrac12 m\langle v^2\rangle \]

The macroscopic pressure is set by the total translational kinetic energy \(K\) of the molecules.

Proof: momentum transfer to a wall

A molecule with \(x\)-velocity \(v_x\) bounces off the right wall and reverses, transferring momentum \(2mv_x\). In time \(dt\), the molecules that reach a wall of area \(A\) are those within distance \(v_x\,dt\) moving towards it: \(\tfrac12(N/V)Av_x\,dt\) of them (the \(\tfrac12\) because half move the other way). Force = momentum per time:

\[ F = \tfrac12\frac{N}{V}Av_x\cdot 2mv_x \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{N}{V}m\langle v_x^2\rangle \]

No direction is special, so \(\langle v_x^2\rangle = \tfrac13\langle v^2\rangle\), giving \(PV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle v^2\rangle\).

2.4  Temperature as kinetic energy: equipartition

Put \(PV = \tfrac23 K\) next to \(PV = Nk_BT\): they agree only if \(K = \tfrac32 Nk_BT\). So temperature measures the average kinetic energy per molecule, \(\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac32k_BT\), shared equally among the three directions of motion. The general rule:

Equipartition theorem

Every independent quadratic term in a molecule's energy (\(\tfrac12mv_x^2\), \(\tfrac12I\omega^2\), \(\tfrac12kx^2\), …) carries on average \(\tfrac12k_BT\). With \(f\) such terms:

\[ U = \frac{f}{2}Nk_BT = \frac{f}{2}nRT \]

GasQuadratic terms \(f\)\(U\) per mole\(c_V\)\(\gamma = c_P/c_V\)
Monatomic (He, Ar)3 translation\(\tfrac32RT\)\(\tfrac32R\)5/3
Diatomic, room \(T\) (N₂, O₂, air)3 translation + 2 rotation\(\tfrac52RT\)\(\tfrac52R\)7/5
Diatomic, high \(T\)+ 2 vibration (kinetic + potential)\(\tfrac72RT\)\(\tfrac72R\)9/7
Counting caution. The slides count a diatomic molecule as having 6 degrees of freedom (3 translation + 2 rotation + 1 vibration). The vibration counts once as a motion but contributes two quadratic terms (kinetic and potential energy), i.e. \(k_BT\). At room temperature the vibration is "frozen out" (a quantum effect), which is why air behaves as \(f = 5\), \(\gamma = 1.4\).
Where it's used later

\(U = \tfrac f2 nRT\) gives \(C_V = \tfrac f2 nR\), \(C_P = C_V + nR\) and \(\gamma\) in Chapter 3. Every adiabatic and engine calculation needs these.

Typical molecular speeds

\[ v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} \]

Lighter molecules move faster at the same temperature. That is the basis of isotope separation by effusion: through a porous barrier, the lighter species leaks out faster, with separation factor \(\sqrt{m_2/m_1}\) per stage. For uranium hexafluoride (\(^{235}\)UF\(_6\) vs \(^{238}\)UF\(_6\), molar masses 349 and 352) that is \(\sqrt{352/349} \approx 1.0043\), so hundreds of stages are needed.

Example · How fast are air molecules?

Find \(v_{\text{rms}}\) for N₂ (\(M = 0.028\ \text{kg/mol}\)) at 300 K.

Show solution

\(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3(8.314)(300)/0.028} \approx 517\ \text{m/s}\), about 1.5 times the speed of sound in air. (Sound travels at a speed of the same order, since it is carried by these molecules.)

2.5  The Maxwell speed distribution

Molecules don't all move at \(v_{\text{rms}}\); their speeds spread out according to

Maxwell speed distribution

\[ f(v) = 4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}v^2\,e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}, \qquad \int_0^\infty f(v)\,dv = 1 \]

\(f(v)\,dv\) is the fraction of molecules with speeds between \(v\) and \(v + dv\). Two competing factors shape it: \(v^2\) (there are more ways to have a large velocity vector — a bigger sphere in velocity space) and \(e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}\) (high energies are exponentially rare).

Three characteristic speeds (always in this order) \[ v_{\text{mp}} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}} \;\lt\; \langle v\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}} \;\lt\; v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} \]

\(v_{\text{mp}}\) is the peak of the curve; the long tail at high speed pulls the mean and the rms above it.

Where the distribution comes from (sketch)

Each velocity component is independently Gaussian: \(f(v_x) \propto e^{-mv_x^2/2k_BT}\), so \(f(\vec v)\,d^3v = \left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}\,d^3v\). Counting all velocity vectors with the same speed \(v\) means integrating over a spherical shell in velocity space, \(d^3v \to 4\pi v^2\,dv\). The peak follows from \(df/dv = 0\), giving \(v_{\text{mp}}\); \(\langle v\rangle\) and \(\langle v^2\rangle\) are standard Gaussian integrals.

2.6  Response coefficients and the partial-derivative toolkit

For liquids and solids there is no simple equation of state. What we can measure instead is how the volume or pressure responds to small changes:

CoefficientDefinitionMeaningIdeal gas
Expansion \(\alpha\)\(\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P\)fractional volume change per kelvin, fixed \(P\)\(1/T\)
Pressure coefficient \(\beta\)\(\frac{1}{P}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V\)fractional pressure change per kelvin, fixed \(V\)\(1/T\)
Isothermal compressibility \(\kappa_T\)\(-\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T\)fractional volume decrease per unit pressure, fixed \(T\)\(1/P\)

(The minus sign makes \(\kappa_T\) positive, since squeezing decreases volume.) Typical solids and liquids have \(\alpha \sim 10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}\) and \(\kappa_T \sim 10^{-10}\ \text{Pa}^{-1}\). If they are roughly constant, the equation of state near a reference point is

\[ V(T,P) \approx V_0\left[1 + \alpha(T - T_0) - \kappa_T(P - P_0)\right] \]

The partial-derivative toolkit

From here on, almost every calculation uses these three facts about a function \(f(x, y)\):

Toolkit

\[ \text{① Total differential:}\quad df = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{\!y}dx + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{\!x}dy \] \[ \text{② Reciprocal:}\quad \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_{\!z} = \frac{1}{(\partial y/\partial x)_z} \qquad \text{③ Cyclic:}\quad \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_{\!z}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_{\!x}\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_{\!y} = -1 \]

The cyclic relation (for three variables tied by one equation, like \(P, V, T\)) says the three response coefficients are not independent:

\[ \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V} = -\frac{(\partial V/\partial T)_P}{(\partial V/\partial P)_T} = \frac{\alpha}{\kappa_T} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \alpha = \kappa_T\,\beta\,P \]

Check with an ideal gas: \(\kappa_T\beta P = \frac1P\cdot\frac1T\cdot P = \frac1T = \alpha\). ✓

Why it matters

A fourth tool, "mixed second derivatives are equal", is what generates the Maxwell relations in Chapter 5. And \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = \alpha/\kappa_T\) reappears in \(C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T\). The habit to build now: write the total differential, then read off or eliminate the coefficient you need.

Example 1 · Heating water in a sealed, rigid bottle

A rigid bottle is completely filled with water (\(\alpha = 2.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), \(\kappa_T = 4.6\times10^{-10}\ \text{Pa}^{-1}\)). By how much does the pressure rise per kelvin of heating?

Show solution

Fixed volume, so we need \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = \alpha/\kappa_T = 2.1\times10^{-4}/4.6\times10^{-10} \approx 4.6\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa/K}\), about 4.5 atm per kelvin. That is why completely full sealed containers burst when warmed.

Example 2 · A wire clamped at both ends (Tut 1)

A wire of length \(L\), cross-section \(A\), tension \(\mathcal F\). Define \(\alpha_1 = \frac1L(\partial L/\partial T)_{\mathcal F}\) and Young's modulus \(E = \frac{L}{A}(\partial\mathcal F/\partial L)_T\), both depending only on \(T\). The ends are fixed and the temperature falls from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\). Find \(\Delta\mathcal F\).

Show solution

Fixed length, so we need \((\partial\mathcal F/\partial T)_L\). Cyclic relation for \(\mathcal F, L, T\):

\[ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal F}{\partial T}\right)_{\!L} = -\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal F}{\partial L}\right)_{\!T}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\mathcal F} = -\frac{EA}{L}\cdot\alpha_1L = -EA\alpha_1 \]

\[ \Delta\mathcal F = -\int_{T_1}^{T_2}EA\alpha_1\,dT \approx EA\alpha_1(T_1 - T_2) \gt 0 \]

Cooling a clamped wire increases its tension (it "wants" to shrink but can't).

Example 3 · 2021 midterm Q1

Show that in a quasi-static process \(dP = \kappa\left(-\frac{dV}{V} + \alpha_V\,dT\right)\), where \(\kappa = -V(\partial P/\partial V)_T\) and \(\alpha_V = \frac1V(\partial V/\partial T)_P\).

Show solution

Total differential of \(P(V,T)\): \(dP = (\partial P/\partial V)_T\,dV + (\partial P/\partial T)_V\,dT\).

First coefficient: \((\partial P/\partial V)_T = -\kappa/V\) by definition of \(\kappa\).

Second: cyclic relation, \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = -(\partial P/\partial V)_T(\partial V/\partial T)_P = (\kappa/V)(V\alpha_V) = \kappa\alpha_V\).

So \(dP = -\kappa\,dV/V + \kappa\alpha_V\,dT\). ∎ (Note \(\kappa = 1/\kappa_T\) is the bulk modulus.)

Chapter 3

Work, Heat and the First Law

What this chapter does

The First Law is energy bookkeeping: the energy of a system changes only by work done on it or heat put into it. The physics is simple; the skill is applying it to processes: computing \(W\), \(Q\) and \(\Delta U\) for each step of a process or an engine cycle. This chapter builds that toolkit.

After this chapter you should be able to
  • use \(\Delta U = W + Q\) with the right sign conventions, and compute work for quasi-static and sudden processes;
  • fill in \(W\), \(Q\), \(\Delta U\) for isothermal, isochoric, isobaric and adiabatic steps of an ideal gas;
  • use the adiabatic relations, and spot when they don't apply;
  • find the efficiency of any cycle built from these steps (Carnot, Otto, Diesel, Joule).

3.1  Internal energy and the First Law

Experiments show that if you take a thermally insulated system from state 1 to state 2, the work needed is always the same, whether you stir it, compress it or pass a current through it. A quantity whose change depends only on the end states is a state function; this one is the internal energy \(U\). If heat can also flow, the same change of state can be produced by any mix of work and heat:

First Law of Thermodynamics

\[ \Delta U = W + Q, \qquad dU = \text{đ}W + \text{đ}Q \]

\(W\) = work done on the system, \(Q\) = heat put into the system.

Sign conventions. This course uses "work done on the system". Some textbooks use work done by the system and write \(\Delta U = Q - W\). Before plugging into any formula, decide whose work it is. In engines we usually quote the work the engine delivers, which is \(-W\).

\(U\) is a state function; \(W\) and \(Q\) are not. The same \(\Delta U\) can be reached with lots of work and little heat or the other way round, which is why small amounts of them are written \(\text{đ}W\), \(\text{đ}Q\).

3.2  Work done on a gas

Push a piston of area \(A\) in by \(dx\) against a gas at pressure \(P\): the work done on the gas is \(PA\,dx = -P\,dV\) (volume decreases, \(dV \lt 0\), work done on the gas is positive).

Quasi-static work

\[ \text{đ}W = -P\,dV, \qquad W = -\int_{V_1}^{V_2}P\,dV \]

On a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram, \(|W|\) is the area under the path. Different paths between the same two states enclose different areas, which is another way of seeing that \(W\) depends on the path.

Sudden processes. If the gas is not in equilibrium (a weight dropped onto a piston, a piston released), its own pressure isn't defined during the process and \(-\int P\,dV\) can't be used. But if the external pressure is constant, the work done on the gas is simply

\[ W = -P_{\text{ext}}\,\Delta V \]
Example · Adding weights one at a time (HW 1)

An ideal gas in a cylinder is kept at constant temperature by a reservoir. Initially \(P_1 = 1\ \text{atm}\), \(V_1\). Three equal weights are placed on the piston one at a time; after the first, the gas settles at \(V_2 = 0.8V_1\). (i) Find each equilibrium state. (ii) Total work done on the gas. (iii) Work when the weights are removed one at a time. Compare with a reversible isothermal compression.

Show solution

States. Isothermal equilibrium: \(PV = P_1V_1\). After weight 1, \(P = P_1V_1/(0.8V_1) = 1.25P_1\), so each weight adds \(0.25P_1\). After 2: \(1.5P_1\), \(V = V_1/1.5 = 0.667V_1\). After 3: \(1.75P_1\), \(V = 0.571V_1\).

Adding weights. During each step the external pressure is the new pressure (constant), so \(W = -P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V\):

\[ W = P_1V_1\left[1.25(1 - 0.8) + 1.5(0.8 - 0.667) + 1.75(0.667 - 0.571)\right] \approx 0.617\,P_1V_1 \]

Removing weights. Now each step expands against the lower pressure: \(W' = -P_1V_1[1.5(0.667 - 0.571) + 1.25(0.8 - 0.667) + 1.0(1 - 0.8)] \approx -0.510\,P_1V_1\).

Compare. Reversible isothermal compression from \(V_1\) to \(V_1/1.75\) needs \(W = P_1V_1\ln 1.75 \approx 0.560\,P_1V_1\). Compressing in jumps costs more work (0.617); expanding in jumps gets less back (0.510). The difference is lost to irreversibility, and \(|W| \ne |W'|\).

3.3  Heat capacity and enthalpy

How much heat it takes to warm something by 1 K depends on what else is happening, so there are two heat capacities:

  • Constant volume: no work is done, so all the heat goes into \(U\): \(C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V\).
  • Constant pressure: the system expands and does work on the surroundings, so more heat is needed. The bookkeeping is neat if we define the enthalpy \(H = U + PV\): at constant \(P\), \(\text{đ}Q = dU + P\,dV = d(U + PV) = dH\).
Heat capacities and enthalpy

\[ C_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V}, \qquad C_P = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P}, \qquad H = U + PV, \qquad Q_P = \Delta H \]

For an ideal gas, \(U = \tfrac f2nRT\) and \(H = U + nRT\) depend only on \(T\), so

Ideal gas

\[ C_P - C_V = nR, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{f+2}{f}, \qquad C_V = \frac{nR}{\gamma - 1}, \quad C_P = \frac{\gamma nR}{\gamma - 1} \]

Monatomic: \(c_V = \tfrac32R\), \(\gamma = 5/3\). Diatomic (air): \(c_V = \tfrac52R\), \(\gamma = 7/5\).

General result: \(C_P - C_V\) for any substance

Write \(U(T,V)\): \(dU = C_V\,dT + (\partial U/\partial V)_T\,dV\). Then \(\text{đ}Q = dU + P\,dV = C_V\,dT + [(\partial U/\partial V)_T + P]\,dV\). Divide by \(dT\) at constant \(P\):

\[ C_P - C_V = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P}\left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{\!T} + P\right] \]

For an ideal gas \((\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0\) and \((\partial V/\partial T)_P = nR/P\), giving \(nR\). Chapter 5 turns this into the more useful \(C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T\).

3.5  Adiabatic processes

In a quasi-static adiabatic process (\(\text{đ}Q = 0\)), the work done on the gas all goes into \(U\): \(C_V\,dT = -P\,dV\). Compress a gas without letting heat out and it heats up; let it expand and it cools. For an ideal gas this integrates to:

Adiabatic relations (ideal gas, quasi-static)

\[ PV^\gamma = \text{const}, \qquad TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}, \qquad TP^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma} = \text{const} \]

Useful forms: \(T_2 = T_1(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1}\) and \(T_2 = T_1(P_2/P_1)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\). On a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram an adiabat is steeper than an isotherm (slope \(-\gamma P/V\) vs \(-P/V\)): during compression the temperature rises too, adding to the pressure rise. Equivalently, a gas is harder to compress adiabatically:

\[ \frac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \gamma \gt 1, \qquad \kappa_S = -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_{\!S} \]
Proof: \(PV^\gamma = \text{const}\)

\(C_V\,dT = -P\,dV\). From \(PV = nRT\), \(dT = (P\,dV + V\,dP)/nR\). Substitute and use \(nR = C_P - C_V\):

\[ C_V(P\,dV + V\,dP) = -(C_P - C_V)P\,dV \;\Rightarrow\; C_PP\,dV + C_VV\,dP = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \gamma\frac{dV}{V} + \frac{dP}{P} = 0 \]

Integrate: \(\gamma\ln V + \ln P = \text{const}\), i.e. \(PV^\gamma = \text{const}\). Substituting \(P = nRT/V\) gives the other two forms.

Watch out: adiabatic ≠ \(PV^\gamma\). \(PV^\gamma = \text{const}\) needs the process to be quasi-static. A free expansion is adiabatic but follows no curve at all (and an ideal gas's \(T\) doesn't change). A piston that is suddenly released and oscillates before settling is adiabatic but not quasi-static. Use energy conservation, not \(PV^\gamma\), as in Example 4 below.
Example 1 · Pumping a bicycle tyre (Tut 2)

1 L of air at 1 atm and 300 K is compressed adiabatically to 7 atm (\(\gamma = 1.4\)). Find (i) the final volume, (ii) the work done, (iii) the final temperature.

Show solution

(i) \(V_2 = V_1(P_1/P_2)^{1/\gamma} = 7^{-1/1.4} = 7^{-5/7} \approx 0.249\ \text{L}\).

(ii) \(W_{\text{on}} = \frac{P_2V_2 - P_1V_1}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{(7)(0.249) - 1}{0.4}\ \text{atm·L} \approx 1.86\ \text{atm·L} \approx 188\ \text{J}\).

(iii) \(T_2 = T_1(P_2/P_1)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 300\times7^{2/7} \approx 523\ \text{K}\). That is why a pump gets hot.

Example 2 · Why it gets colder with altitude (Tut 2)

Rising air expands adiabatically as the pressure drops. (i) Show \(dT/dP = \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\frac TP\). (ii) Combine with the barometric equation to find \(dT/dz\) for air.

Show solution

(i) Differentiate \(TP^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma} = \text{const}\) logarithmically: \(\frac{dT}{T} + \frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\frac{dP}{P} = 0\).

(ii) Barometric: \(dP/dz = -MgP/(RT)\). Chain rule:

\[ \frac{dT}{dz} = \frac{dT}{dP}\frac{dP}{dz} = -\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\frac{Mg}{R} = -\frac{0.4}{1.4}\cdot\frac{0.029\times9.8}{8.314} \approx -9.8\ \text{K/km} \]

Example 3 · Polytropic process \(PV^m = C\) (HW 1)

An ideal gas goes from \((P_1,V_1)\) to \((P_2,V_2)\) along \(PV^m = C\). (i) Find the work done by the gas. (ii) Show its heat capacity along this path is \(C_m = \frac{m-\gamma}{m-1}C_V\).

Show solution

(i) \(\int P\,dV = C\int V^{-m}dV = \frac{C(V_2^{1-m} - V_1^{1-m})}{1-m} = \frac{P_1V_1 - P_2V_2}{m - 1}\) (using \(CV^{1-m} = PV\)).

(ii) \(\text{đ}Q = C_V\,dT + P\,dV\). Along the path \(TV^{m-1} = \text{const}\), so \(dV/V = -dT/[(m-1)T]\) and \(P\,dV = nRT\,dV/V = -nR\,dT/(m-1)\). Hence \(C_m = C_V - \frac{nR}{m-1} = C_V - \frac{(\gamma-1)C_V}{m-1} = \frac{m-\gamma}{m-1}C_V\).

Check: \(m = 0\) (isobaric) gives \(\gamma C_V = C_P\) ✓; \(m = \gamma\) (adiabatic) gives 0 ✓; \(m = 1\) (isothermal) gives \(\infty\) ✓ (heat flows with no temperature change).

Example 4 · A heavy piston released (2021 midterm Q4)

\(n\) mol of ideal gas (\(V_0\), \(T_0\)) is in an insulated vertical cylinder under a piston of mass \(M\), area \(A\), with atmospheric pressure \(p_a\) above. The piston is released, oscillates, and comes to rest. Find the final \(T\) and \(V\).

Show solution

Not quasi-static (the piston oscillates), so don't use \(PV^\gamma\). Use energy. The final pressure balances the piston: \(P_{\text{eq}} = p_a + Mg/A\). The atmosphere and gravity together act like a constant external pressure \(P_{\text{eq}}\), and the piston's kinetic energy is eventually dissipated into the gas. So the work done on the gas is \(-P_{\text{eq}}(V_f - V_0)\) and \(Q = 0\):

\[ nc_V(T_f - T_0) = -P_{\text{eq}}(V_f - V_0), \qquad P_{\text{eq}}V_f = nRT_f \]

Substitute \(P_{\text{eq}}V_f = nRT_f\) and use \(c_V + R = c_P\):

\[ T_f = \frac{c_VT_0 + P_{\text{eq}}V_0/n}{c_P}, \qquad V_f = \frac{nRT_f}{P_{\text{eq}}} \]

3.6  Free expansion and throttling

Two processes in which a gas expands with no heat exchanged, each conserving a different energy-like quantity:

ProcessSetupConservedCoefficientIdeal gas
Joule (free expansion)gas rushes into a vacuum in a rigid, insulated box: \(W = Q = 0\)\(U\)\(\lambda = (\partial T/\partial V)_U\)\(\lambda = 0\)
Joule–Thomson (throttling)gas pushed slowly through a porous plug from \(P_1\) to \(P_2\), insulated\(H\)\(\mu = (\partial T/\partial P)_H\)\(\mu = 0\)

For an ideal gas \(U\) and \(H\) depend only on \(T\), so neither process changes its temperature. Real gases usually cool in a free expansion: as the molecules spread out they do work against their mutual attraction. In throttling they can cool (\(\mu \gt 0\), used in refrigerators and gas liquefaction) or warm (\(\mu \lt 0\)), depending on \(T\) and \(P\). Chapter 5 gives formulas for both.

Why throttling conserves \(H\)

Follow a fixed amount of gas through the plug. On the high-pressure side, the gas behind it pushes it in with work \(P_1V_1\); on the low-pressure side it must push the gas ahead out of the way, doing work \(P_2V_2\). No heat flows, so \(U_2 - U_1 = P_1V_1 - P_2V_2\), i.e. \(U_1 + P_1V_1 = U_2 + P_2V_2\): \(H_1 = H_2\). Relations: \((\partial U/\partial V)_T = -\lambda C_V\) and \((\partial H/\partial P)_T = -\mu C_P\).

3.7  Heat engines and the Carnot cycle

A heat engine runs in a cycle: it absorbs heat \(Q_H\) from a hot reservoir, delivers work \(W\), and dumps heat \(Q_C\) into a cold reservoir. Over a full cycle the working gas returns to its starting state, so \(\Delta U = 0\) and \(W = Q_H - Q_C\) (all quantities as magnitudes). A refrigerator runs the same cycle backwards, using work to pump heat from cold to hot.

Figures of merit

\[ \eta = \frac{W}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{Q_C}{Q_H} \quad(\text{engine}), \qquad \text{COP} = \frac{Q_C}{W} = \frac{Q_C}{Q_H - Q_C} \quad(\text{refrigerator}) \]

The Carnot cycle is the ideal reversible engine: two isotherms joined by two adiabats.

Carnot efficiency

\[ \eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}, \qquad \frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = \frac{T_C}{T_H}, \qquad \text{COP}_{\text{Carnot}} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C} \]

The efficiency depends only on the two temperatures and is always below 1. A refrigerator's COP can exceed 1: it moves more heat than the work it uses, which is fine because the heat dumped is \(Q_H = Q_C + W\). Chapter 4 shows that no engine between \(T_H\) and \(T_C\) can beat \(\eta_{\text{Carnot}}\).

Proof: \(\eta = 1 - T_C/T_H\)

Isothermal steps: \(Q_H = nRT_H\ln(V_B/V_A)\), \(Q_C = nRT_C\ln(V_C/V_D)\). Adiabats: \(T_HV_B^{\gamma-1} = T_CV_C^{\gamma-1}\) and \(T_HV_A^{\gamma-1} = T_CV_D^{\gamma-1}\). Dividing, \(V_B/V_A = V_C/V_D\), so the logarithms cancel: \(Q_C/Q_H = T_C/T_H\).

Recipe for the efficiency of any cycle

① Label the corners and identify each step's type. ② Find the steps where heat goes in (\(Q_H\)) and out (\(Q_C\)), using the process table: \(C_V\Delta T\) for isochoric, \(C_P\Delta T\) for isobaric, \(nRT\ln\) for isothermal, 0 for adiabatic. ③ \(\eta = 1 - |Q_C|/Q_H\). ④ Use the adiabatic relations to eliminate temperatures in favour of the given volumes or pressures.
Example 1 · Otto cycle (petrol engine, Tut 3)

Two adiabats and two isochores, compression ratio \(r = V_1/V_2\). Show \(\eta = 1 - r^{1-\gamma}\).

Show solution

Heat in on the isochore 2→3: \(Q_H = C_V(T_3 - T_2)\). Heat out on the isochore 4→1: \(Q_C = C_V(T_4 - T_1)\). Adiabats between the same two volumes: \(T_2 = T_1r^{\gamma-1}\), \(T_3 = T_4r^{\gamma-1}\). So \(T_3 - T_2 = r^{\gamma-1}(T_4 - T_1)\) and

\[ \eta = 1 - \frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2} = 1 - r^{1-\gamma} \]

Higher compression ratio, higher efficiency: \(r = 8\), \(\gamma = 1.4\) gives \(\eta \approx 56\%\).

Example 2 · Two isobars + two adiabats (Joule/Brayton cycle, HW 1)

An ideal-gas engine runs between pressures \(p_1\) (high) and \(p_2\) (low) on two isobars joined by adiabats. Find \(\eta\).

Show solution

Heat in on the high-pressure isobar: \(Q_H = C_P(T_b - T_a)\). Heat out on the low-pressure isobar: \(Q_C = C_P(T_c - T_d)\). Each adiabat connects \(p_1\) to \(p_2\), so \(T_c = T_b\,(p_2/p_1)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\) and \(T_d = T_a\,(p_2/p_1)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\). Therefore

\[ \eta = 1 - \frac{T_c - T_d}{T_b - T_a} = 1 - \left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} \]

Example 3 · Diesel cycle (2021 midterm Q2)

1→2 adiabatic compression, 2→3 isobaric, 3→4 adiabatic expansion, 4→1 isochoric (\(V_4 = V_1\)). Express \(\eta\) in terms of \(\gamma\), \(V_1\), \(V_2\), \(V_3\).

Show solution

Heat in (isobaric 2→3): \(Q_H = C_P(T_3 - T_2)\). Heat out (isochoric 4→1): \(Q_C = C_V(T_4 - T_1)\). So \(\eta = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2}\).

Express everything via \(T_1\): \(T_2 = T_1(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1}\); isobaric \(T_3 = T_2V_3/V_2\); adiabat \(T_4 = T_3(V_3/V_1)^{\gamma-1}\). Then \(T_4 - T_1 = T_1[(V_3/V_2)^\gamma - 1]\) and \(T_3 - T_2 = T_1(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1}(V_3 - V_2)/V_2\). Dividing:

\[ \eta = 1 - \frac{V_3^{\gamma} - V_2^{\gamma}}{\gamma\,V_1^{\gamma-1}(V_3 - V_2)} \]

Chapter 4

Entropy and the Second Law

What this chapter does

The First Law would allow a hot cup of tea to get hotter by drawing heat from a cold room, as long as energy is conserved. That never happens. The Second Law captures which way things actually go, and this chapter turns it into a number, the entropy \(S\): in any process that happens by itself, the total entropy goes up. The main practical skill is computing \(\Delta S\), which the exams test heavily.

After this chapter you should be able to
  • state the Second Law (Kelvin, Clausius) and explain why no engine beats Carnot;
  • compute \(\Delta S\) for heating, phase changes, reservoirs, gas expansions and mixing, including irreversible processes;
  • decide whether a process is reversible by computing \(\Delta S_{\text{universe}}\);
  • use \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV\) and \(S = k_B\ln\Omega\).

4.1  Reversible processes and reservoirs

Two idealisations make the theory work, in the way frictionless planes make mechanics work:

  • A reversible process is quasi-static with no dissipation (§1.2). Run it backwards and both the system and the surroundings return to exactly where they were. Real processes only approximate this.
  • A heat reservoir has such a large heat capacity that taking heat from it or adding heat to it doesn't change its temperature (the atmosphere, a lake, a big water bath). Heat exchange is always reversible from the reservoir's point of view.

4.2  The Second Law

Two everyday observations, stated carefully, turn out to be the same law:

Kelvin statement

No process can have as its only effect the complete conversion of heat into work.

Clausius statement

Heat cannot flow from a colder body to a hotter one with no other effect.

The words "only" and "no other effect" matter. An ideal gas expanding isothermally converts heat fully into work, but its volume has changed (another effect), so it can't be repeated in a cycle. A refrigerator moves heat from cold to hot, but it consumes work. What is forbidden is a cyclic machine that turns heat from a single reservoir entirely into work, or heat flowing uphill by itself.

Proof: the two statements are equivalent

Violate Clausius ⇒ violate Kelvin. If heat could flow from cold to hot by itself, run a Carnot engine between the reservoirs and send the heat it dumps into the cold reservoir straight back up to the hot one. Net effect: heat taken from the hot reservoir and turned entirely into work. Kelvin is violated.

Violate Kelvin ⇒ violate Clausius. If a perfect engine could turn heat fully into work, use that work to run a refrigerator. Net effect: heat moved from cold to hot with nothing else changed. Clausius is violated.

4.3  Carnot's theorem and absolute temperature

Carnot's theorem

No engine working between two reservoirs is more efficient than a reversible one, and all reversible engines between the same two temperatures have the same efficiency:

\[ \eta \le \eta_{\text{rev}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} \]

This is the ultimate speed limit for turning heat into work, and it doesn't depend on the working substance (gas, steam, rubber band) or the details of the engine. Because it is universal, it can be used to define temperature without reference to any material: the thermodynamic temperature is defined by \(Q_C/Q_H = T_C/T_H\) for a reversible engine. It agrees with the ideal-gas scale of §1.5 (both give the Carnot result).

Proof sketch

Suppose an engine X beat a reversible engine R between the same reservoirs. Run R backwards as a refrigerator, driven by part of X's work, and let it put back into the hot reservoir the heat X took out. Because X is more efficient, there is work left over, and the only net effect is heat taken from the cold reservoir and turned into work, or heat moved from cold to hot. That contradicts the Second Law. Applying this argument both ways to two reversible engines shows their efficiencies must be equal.

For the temperature scale: if \(Q_C/Q_H = f(\theta_C, \theta_H)\) for every reversible engine, chaining two engines through an intermediate reservoir forces \(f(\theta_C, \theta_H) = g(\theta_C)/g(\theta_H)\). Choosing \(g(\theta) = \theta\) defines the absolute scale.

4.4  Entropy

For a Carnot cycle, \(Q_H/T_H = Q_C/T_C\); with signs (heat absorbed positive), \(Q_H/T_H + Q_C/T_C = 0\). This generalises to any reversible cycle broken into many small heat exchanges: \(\oint\text{đ}Q_{\text{rev}}/T = 0\). An integral that vanishes around every closed loop doesn't depend on the path between two points, so it defines a state function:

Definition of entropy

\[ \Delta S = S_B - S_A = \int_A^B\frac{\text{đ}Q_{\text{rev}}}{T} \]

Two things to hold on to:

  1. The integral must be taken along a reversible path. For a real (irreversible) process, \(\int\text{đ}Q/T\) is not \(\Delta S\).
  2. But \(\Delta S\) itself only depends on the end states. So for an irreversible process, invent any convenient reversible path between the same start and end, and integrate along that. This is the single most important trick in the chapter.
Proof: Clausius's theorem \(\oint\text{đ}Q/T \le 0\)

Let a system go round any cycle, exchanging heat \(\text{đ}Q\) at temperatures \(T\). Supply each bit of heat with a small Carnot engine running from a single big reservoir at \(T_0\); each engine draws \(T_0\,\text{đ}Q/T\) from that reservoir. After one cycle the system and all the small engines are back to their start, and the net heat drawn from the single reservoir, \(T_0\oint\text{đ}Q/T\), has become work. Kelvin forbids this from being positive, so \(\oint\text{đ}Q/T \le 0\), with equality for a reversible cycle (run it backwards and the inequality flips).

4.5  The entropy principle

Compare a real process with a reversible one between the same states and Clausius's theorem gives

Clausius inequality and the entropy principle

\[ dS \ge \frac{\text{đ}Q}{T} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta S_{\text{isolated}} \ge 0, \qquad \Delta S_{\text{univ}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} \ge 0 \]

Equality holds only for reversible processes. In words: the entropy of an isolated system never decreases; treating "system + surroundings" as one isolated whole, the entropy of the universe never decreases. This gives a mechanical test:

Reversibility test: compute \(\Delta S_{\text{sys}}\) and \(\Delta S_{\text{surr}}\) separately and add them. \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} = 0\) ⇒ reversible; \(\gt 0\) ⇒ irreversible; \(\lt 0\) ⇒ impossible (check your signs).
Proof

Go from A to B by an irreversible path I, and come back by a reversible path II. For the cycle, \(\int_I\text{đ}Q/T + \int_{II}\text{đ}Q/T \le 0\). The return leg is reversible, so \(\int_{II}\text{đ}Q/T = S_A - S_B\). Hence \(\int_I\text{đ}Q/T \le S_B - S_A\). For an isolated system \(\text{đ}Q = 0\), so \(S_B \ge S_A\).

Example 1 · Water heated by a reservoir (HW 2)

1 kg of water at 0 °C is dropped into a reservoir at 100 °C (\(c = 4.18\ \text{J/(g·K)}\)). Find \(\Delta S\) of the water, of the reservoir, and of the universe. Is it reversible? How could it be done reversibly?

Show solution

Water: \(\Delta S = mc\ln(T_2/T_1) = 4180\ln(373.15/273.15) \approx +1304\ \text{J/K}\).

Reservoir: it gives up \(Q = mc\Delta T = 4.18\times10^5\ \text{J}\) at 373.15 K: \(\Delta S = -Q/T = -1120\ \text{J/K}\).

Universe: \(+184\ \text{J/K} \gt 0\), irreversible (heat crossed a finite temperature gap). A reversible version: put the water in contact with a long series of reservoirs, each only slightly hotter than the last, from 0 °C up to 100 °C. Then every heat transfer happens across an infinitesimal gap and \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} \to 0\).

Example 2 · Two blocks reaching a common temperature

Two identical blocks, heat capacity \(C\), at \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), are put in contact (insulated from everything else). Find \(\Delta S\).

Show solution

Energy conservation: \(T_f = (T_1 + T_2)/2\). Each block is "heated" or "cooled" from its start to \(T_f\):

\[ \Delta S = C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_1} + C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_2} = C\ln\frac{(T_1 + T_2)^2}{4T_1T_2} \ge 0 \]

Always non-negative since \((T_1 + T_2)^2 \ge 4T_1T_2\) (AM ≥ GM), with equality only when \(T_1 = T_2\). For unequal masses use \(T_f = \sum m_ic_iT_i/\sum m_ic_i\).

Example 3 · Mixing bath water (Tut 4)

50 L of water at 55 °C is mixed with 25 L at 10 °C. How much entropy is created?

Show solution

\(T_f = (50\times328.15 + 25\times283.15)/75 = 313.15\ \text{K}\) (40 °C).

\[ \Delta S = (5.0\times10^4)(4.186)\ln\frac{313.15}{328.15} + (2.5\times10^4)(4.186)\ln\frac{313.15}{283.15} \approx -9.79 + 10.54 = +0.75\ \text{kJ/K} \]

The hot water loses entropy, the cold water gains more: net creation, as it must be.

Example 4 · Free expansion of an ideal gas

An ideal gas expands into a vacuum from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) in an insulated box. Find \(\Delta S\) of the gas and of the universe.

Show solution

\(W = Q = 0\), so \(\Delta U = 0\) and \(T\) is unchanged. No heat flowed, but that does not mean \(\Delta S = 0\): the process is irreversible, so \(\int\text{đ}Q/T\) is not \(\Delta S\). Replace it by a reversible isothermal expansion between the same states:

\[ \Delta S_{\text{gas}} = nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} \gt 0 \]

The surroundings are untouched, so \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} = nR\ln(V_2/V_1) \gt 0\). Compare a reversible isothermal expansion: the gas gains the same \(\Delta S\), but the reservoir loses exactly that much, giving \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} = 0\).

Example 5 · Heating air at constant pressure (Tut 4)

1 L of air (diatomic) at 300 K and 1 bar is heated at constant pressure until its volume doubles. Find \(\Delta S\).

Show solution

Constant \(P\), so volume doubling means \(T\) doubles. \(\Delta S = nc_P\ln 2\) with \(n = PV/RT\) and \(c_P = \tfrac72R\):

\[ \Delta S = \frac{PV}{T}\cdot\frac72\ln2 = \frac{(10^5)(10^{-3})}{300}\cdot3.5\cdot0.693 \approx 0.81\ \text{J/K} \]

Example 6 · A resistor in a heat bath (Tut 4)

A 100 Ω resistor is held at 300 K by its surroundings while 10 A flows for 300 s. Find \(\Delta S\) of the resistor and of the universe.

Show solution

The resistor ends in the same state it started (same \(T\)), so \(\Delta S_{\text{resistor}} = 0\): entropy is a state function. The electrical work \(I^2Rt = 3\times10^{6}\ \text{J}\) is all dumped as heat into the surroundings at 300 K:

\[ \Delta S_{\text{univ}} = \frac{3\times10^6}{300} = 1.0\times10^4\ \text{J/K} \]

Example 7 · Minimum work to cool one body (Tut 3)

Two identical bodies (heat capacity \(C_P\)) start at \(T_i\). A refrigerator working between them cools one to \(T_2\). What is the minimum work needed?

Show solution

Minimum work ⟺ reversible ⟺ \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} = 0\). If the hot body ends at \(T_1\):

\[ C_P\ln\frac{T_2}{T_i} + C_P\ln\frac{T_1}{T_i} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; T_1 = \frac{T_i^2}{T_2} \]

Energy: the work equals heat into the hot body minus heat out of the cold one:

\[ W_{\min} = C_P(T_1 - T_i) - C_P(T_i - T_2) = C_P\left(\frac{T_i^2}{T_2} + T_2 - 2T_i\right) \]

Example 8 · Liquid vs solid just below the melting point (HW 2)

A substance has molar heat capacities \(c_s\) (solid) and \(c_l\) (liquid), melting point \(T_0\) and molar latent heat \(l\). At a temperature \(T\) slightly below \(T_0\), find \(S_{\text{liquid}} - S_{\text{solid}}\).

Show solution

Entropy is a state function, so go round a convenient loop: start from liquid at \(T\), warm it to \(T_0\), freeze it, cool the solid back to \(T\).

\[ S_l(T) - S_s(T) = \underbrace{\frac{l}{T_0}}_{\text{at } T_0} + c_l\ln\frac{T}{T_0} - c_s\ln\frac{T}{T_0} = \frac{l}{T_0} + (c_l - c_s)\ln\frac{T}{T_0} \]

See also the ice-cube example in §9.3, which combines melting, warming and a reservoir.

4.7  The fundamental relation and ideal-gas entropy

For a reversible change, \(\text{đ}Q = T\,dS\) and \(\text{đ}W = -P\,dV\), so the First Law becomes

Fundamental relation

\[ dU = T\,dS - P\,dV \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad dS = \frac{1}{T}\,dU + \frac{P}{T}\,dV \]

It contains only state functions, so although derived for a reversible change, it holds between any two nearby equilibrium states. Reading off coefficients (§2.6, tool ①):

\[ \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)_{\!V} = \frac1T, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{\!U} = \frac PT \]
Where it's used later

This one line is the starting point of Chapters 5–9. Chapter 5 rewrites it with \(T\) and \(P\) as variables (the potentials \(F\) and \(G\)); Chapter 6 uses \(\partial S/\partial U = 1/T\) to show that heat flows from hot to cold.

For an ideal gas, \(dU = nc_V\,dT\) and \(P/T = nR/V\), so

Ideal-gas entropy change

\[ \Delta S = nc_V\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = nc_P\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - nR\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} \]

Proof of the \((T,P)\) form

From \(PV = nRT\): \(dV/V = dT/T - dP/P\). Substituting into \(dS = nc_V\,dT/T + nR\,dV/V\) and using \(c_V + R = c_P\) gives \(dS = nc_P\,dT/T - nR\,dP/P\).

Beyond the ideal gas. For any substance with equation of state \(P(T,V)\):

\[ dS = \frac{C_V}{T}\,dT + \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V}dV \]

(proved neatly in §5.6 with a Maxwell relation). For a van der Waals gas, \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = nR/(V - nb)\), so \(\Delta S = C_V\ln(T_2/T_1) + nR\ln\frac{V_2 - nb}{V_1 - nb}\). The attraction \(a\) changes the energy but not the entropy at fixed \(T\) and \(V\).

4.8  What entropy measures: \(S = k_B\ln\Omega\)

Flip 100 coins. A microstate is the exact sequence (there are \(2^{100}\)); a macrostate is what you'd actually report, say "\(n\) heads". The number of microstates in a macrostate is the multiplicity \(\Omega(N,n) = \binom{N}{n}\). "All heads" has \(\Omega = 1\); "50 heads" has \(\Omega \approx 10^{29}\). If the coins are shaken randomly, you will essentially always see something near 50, simply because overwhelmingly more microstates look like that.

Boltzmann entropy

\[ S = k_B\ln\Omega \]

Equilibrium is the macrostate with the largest \(\Omega\). The Second Law becomes a statement about probability: systems drift towards macrostates with more microstates because those are vastly more likely. For a macroscopic system (\(N \sim 10^{23}\)) "vastly" is so extreme that a decrease in entropy is never observed.

Sackur–Tetrode equation (monatomic ideal gas)

\[ S = Nk_B\left[\ln\left(\frac VN\left(\frac{4\pi mU}{3Nh^2}\right)^{3/2}\right) + \frac52\right] \]

Differentiate and the familiar results drop out: \(1/T = \partial S/\partial U = \tfrac32Nk_B/U\) gives \(U = \tfrac32Nk_BT\); \(P/T = \partial S/\partial V = Nk_B/V\) gives \(PV = Nk_BT\). Microscopic counting and macroscopic thermodynamics agree.

Where Sackur–Tetrode comes from (sketch)

\(\Omega\) is proportional to the volume of phase space available: positions contribute \(V^N\); momenta lie on a \(3N\)-dimensional sphere of radius \(\sqrt{2mU}\), contributing \(\propto U^{3N/2}\); and dividing by \(N!\) accounts for identical particles being indistinguishable. So \(\Omega \propto V^NU^{3N/2}/[N!\,(3N/2)!]\). Taking the log with Stirling's approximation \(\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N\) gives the formula. The \(1/N!\) is what makes \(S\) depend on \(V/N\) rather than \(V\); see the Gibbs paradox in §8.1.

Thermodynamic Potentials

What this chapter does

\(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV\) is elegant but awkward to use. Its variables are \(S\) and \(V\), while in a real experiment we control temperature and pressure (a water bath, an open beaker in the atmosphere). Entropy can be neither controlled nor read off a dial. This chapter builds the tools to fix that:

  • three new "energy functions" \(H, F, G\) whose variables include \(T\) and \(P\);
  • the Maxwell relations, which replace derivatives of entropy by derivatives of \(P, V, T\) that we can measure;
  • the chemical potential \(\mu\), for systems whose particle number can change.
After this chapter you should be able to
  • write down the four potentials and their differentials, and read \(S, P, V, T\) off them;
  • derive any Maxwell relation in ten seconds and use it to get rid of a \(\partial S\);
  • derive \(C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T\) and put numbers into it.

5.1  Natural variables: what a differential tells you

Look at the structure of \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV\): each term on the right is "some quantity × d(another quantity)". The quantities after the \(d\), here \(S\) and \(V\), are the natural variables of \(U\). Treat \(U\) as \(U(S,V)\), compare with the total differential \(dU = (\partial U/\partial S)_V dS + (\partial U/\partial V)_S dV\), and read off:

Reading \(T\) and \(P\) off \(U(S,V)\)

\[ T = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{\!V}, \qquad P = -\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{\!S} \]

This says something important: if you know \(U\) as a function of its natural variables \(S\) and \(V\), you can find the temperature, the pressure and hence the equation of state. All the thermodynamics of the system is contained in that one function. That is where the word "potential" comes from: just as differentiating a potential energy gives a force, differentiating a thermodynamic potential gives \(T\), \(P\) and so on.

Example · The whole ideal gas from \(U(S,V)\)

The entropy of a monatomic ideal gas (Chapter 4) can be inverted to give \(U(S,V) = c\,V^{-2/3}\,e^{2S/3Nk_B}\), with \(c\) a constant. Find \(T\) and \(P\) and check that you recover familiar results.

Show solution

\[ T = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{\!V} = \frac{2}{3Nk_B}\,U \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_BT \]

\[ P = -\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{\!S} = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{U}{V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PV = \tfrac{2}{3}U = Nk_BT \]

Equipartition and the ideal gas law both come out of a single function.

5.2  Changing variables: enthalpy, Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies

To swap the natural variable \(S\) for \(T\), there is a standard trick using the product rule \(d(TS) = T\,dS + S\,dT\). Subtract \(d(TS)\) from \(dU\):

\[ d(U - TS) = T\,dS - P\,dV - T\,dS - S\,dT = -S\,dT - P\,dV \]

The \(T\,dS\) term cancels and is replaced by \(-S\,dT\). The new function \(U - TS\) has natural variables \(T\) and \(V\). In the same way, adding \(d(PV)\) swaps \(V\) for \(P\). This gives four functions in total (the operation is called a Legendre transformation):

The four thermodynamic potentials

\[ \begin{aligned} &U &&dU = T\,dS - P\,dV &&\text{natural variables } S, V \\ &H = U + PV &&dH = T\,dS + V\,dP &&\text{natural variables } S, P \\ &F = U - TS &&dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV &&\text{natural variables } T, V \\ &G = U - TS + PV &&dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP &&\text{natural variables } T, P \end{aligned} \]

No need to memorise four differentials, just the pattern: adding \(PV\) turns \(-P\,dV\) into \(+V\,dP\); subtracting \(TS\) turns \(T\,dS\) into \(-S\,dT\).

Reading off coefficients works for every potential:

\[ \begin{aligned} &T = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial S}\right)_P, &&V = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\right)_S \\ &S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_V, &&P = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_T \\ &S = -\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_P, &&V = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_T \end{aligned} \]

The last line gets used constantly in Chapters 7 and 8: the \(T\)-derivative of \(G\) is \(-S\), the \(P\)-derivative is \(V\).

Memory aid: the thermodynamic square (optional)

Write the four variables at the corners and the four potentials on the sides ("Valid Facts and Theoretical Understandings Generate Solutions to Hard Problems", read clockwise from top-left):

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} V & F & T \\ U & & G \\ S & H & P \end{array} \]

Add two diagonal arrows, one from \(S\) to \(T\) and one from \(P\) to \(V\). Then:

  • Each potential's natural variables are the two corners next to it (\(F\): \(V\) and \(T\)).
  • In its differential, each natural variable is multiplied by the corner diagonally opposite.
  • The sign is \(+\) if the arrow points away from the natural variable, \(-\) if it points towards it.

Check with \(G\): natural variables \(T, P\). Opposite \(T\) is \(S\), and the arrow points towards \(T\), giving \(-S\,dT\). Opposite \(P\) is \(V\), and the arrow points away from \(P\), giving \(+V\,dP\). If this feels fiddly, the "add \(PV\) / subtract \(TS\)" rule above is more direct.

5.3  What the new functions mean physically

\(H\), \(F\) and \(G\) are not just a change of variables; each one is the useful quantity in a particular experimental situation.

Enthalpy \(H\): the heat absorbed at constant pressure

At constant pressure \(dP = 0\), so \(dH = T\,dS = \text{đ}Q\):

Heat at constant pressure

\[ Q_P = \Delta H \]

Chemical reactions, melting and boiling almost always happen at atmospheric pressure, so the "heat of reaction" or "latent heat" you find in tables is really a \(\Delta H\).

Where it's used later

The latent heat \(L = \Delta H = T\Delta S\) in Chapter 7; the reaction heat \(\Delta h_0\) in the van 't Hoff equation of Chapter 8; and throttling (Joule–Thomson, Chapter 3) is a constant-\(H\) process.

Helmholtz free energy \(F\): the maximum work at constant temperature

For a system in contact with a reservoir at temperature \(T\), the work it can deliver is at most \(-\Delta F\) (proved in Chapter 6). In a reversible isothermal process, the work done on the system equals \(\Delta F\) exactly:

Reversible isothermal process

\[ W_{\text{on}} = \Delta F, \qquad W_{\text{by, max}} = -\Delta F \]

Why not \(-\Delta U\)? Because during an isothermal process the system also exchanges heat \(T\Delta S\) with the reservoir. Only the part \(F = U - TS\) is "free" to become work; the rest, \(TS\), can only be exchanged as heat.

Example · Isothermal expansion of an ideal gas

\(n\) mol of ideal gas expands reversibly and isothermally at temperature \(T\) from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\). Find \(\Delta F\) and compare it with the work done by the gas.

Show solution

For an ideal gas \(U\) depends only on \(T\), so \(\Delta U = 0\); the entropy change is \(\Delta S = nR\ln(V_2/V_1)\). Hence

\[ \Delta F = \Delta U - T\Delta S = -nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} \]

The work done by the gas is \(\int P\,dV = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1) = -\Delta F\). They match: every joule of work came from heat drawn from the reservoir. The gas's \(U\) is unchanged, but its \(F\) went down.

Gibbs free energy \(G\): which way a process goes at constant \(T\) and \(P\)

Most real processes (reactions, phase changes, dissolving) take place at constant temperature and pressure. Chapter 6 shows that under these conditions a process can only go in the direction that lowers \(G\), and equilibrium is the minimum of \(G\). That makes \(G\) the central quantity of Chapters 6–8.

Why it exists

Questions like "does ice melt at −5 °C?" or "does this reaction run by itself?" are answered by the sign of \(\Delta G\). The form \(G = H - TS\) shows the competition: lowering \(H\) (releasing heat) favours one direction, raising \(S\) (more disorder) may favour the other, and the temperature \(T\) sets which one wins.

Proof: why "maximum \(S\)" and "minimum \(U\)" are the same statement

An isolated system at fixed \(U\) has maximum \(S\). Let \(X\) be any internal variable that is free to change; then \((\partial S/\partial X)_U = 0\) and \((\partial^2 S/\partial X^2)_U \lt 0\).

Let \(\Gamma = (\partial U/\partial X)_S\). By the cyclic relation, \(\Gamma = -(\partial U/\partial S)_X(\partial S/\partial X)_U = -T(\partial S/\partial X)_U = 0\). Differentiating once more:

\[ \left(\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial X^2}\right)_{\!S} = -T\left(\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial X^2}\right)_{\!U} \gt 0 \]

So at fixed \(S\), the same state minimises \(U\). That is why equilibrium can be phrased either as "entropy maximum" or as "energy (potential) minimum".

5.4  Maxwell relations: trading entropy for measurable quantities

The problem: many questions ask for an entropy change, e.g. "how much does the entropy of a copper block change when it is compressed at constant temperature?" That needs \((\partial S/\partial P)_T\), but there is no "entropy meter". The Maxwell relations replace such entropy derivatives by derivatives of \(P, V, T\), which come from the equation of state or from tabulated data.

Where they come from is simple. Each potential is a state function, so its mixed second derivative does not depend on the order of differentiation (the toolkit in §2.6). Take \(dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP\): the coefficients are \(-S\) and \(V\), and cross-differentiating gives

\[ \left.\frac{\partial(-S)}{\partial P}\right|_T = \left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right|_P \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_{\!T} = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} \]

Doing this once for each potential:

Maxwell relations

\[ \begin{aligned} \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_{\!S} &= -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_{\!V} &&(U) \\ \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_{\!S} &= \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_{\!P} &&(H) \\ \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{\!T} &= \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V} &&(F) \\ \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_{\!T} &= -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} &&(G) \end{aligned} \]

The ones you will actually use are the last two (from \(F\) and \(G\)): their left sides ask "how does entropy change with \(V\) or \(P\) at constant \(T\)?", and their right sides come straight from the equation of state. You don't need to memorise them: write the relevant differential, cross-differentiate, and the signs take care of themselves.

Example 1 · Sanity check: isothermal expansion of an ideal gas

Use a Maxwell relation to re-derive \(\Delta S = nR\ln(V_2/V_1)\) for the isothermal expansion of an ideal gas.

Show solution

At constant \(T\), \(dS = (\partial S/\partial V)_T\,dV\). The \(F\) relation gives \((\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V\), and for an ideal gas \(P = nRT/V\), so \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = nR/V\):

\[ \Delta S = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{nR}{V}\,dV = nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} \]

Same answer as Chapter 4's \(\int\text{đ}Q/T\), but without ever asking how much heat flowed.

Example 2 · Compressing a litre of water

At 20 °C, 1 L of water is compressed isothermally from 1 atm to 101 atm. Its expansion coefficient is \(\alpha = 2.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), and its volume barely changes. Find the entropy change of the water and the heat it gives out.

Show solution

Use the \(G\) relation: \((\partial S/\partial P)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P = -V\alpha\). The liquid's volume is nearly constant, so treat \(V\alpha\) as constant:

\[ \Delta S \approx -V\alpha\,\Delta P = -(10^{-3})(2.1\times10^{-4})(1.013\times10^{7}) \approx -2.1\ \text{J/K} \]

For a reversible isothermal process \(Q = T\Delta S \approx 293 \times (-2.1) \approx -6.2\times10^{2}\ \text{J}\): about 620 J of heat is released. No entropy was measured, only one tabulated value of \(\alpha\).

5.5  The relation between \(C_P\) and \(C_V\)

For solids and liquids, heating at constant volume is practically impossible: the material wants to expand, and holding its volume fixed takes enormous pressure. So experiments measure \(C_P\). When theory needs \(C_V\), it is calculated from \(C_P\), \(\alpha\) and \(\kappa_T\) using:

\(C_P - C_V\)

\[ C_P - C_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} \]

  • Every factor on the right is \(\geq 0\) (\(\kappa_T \gt 0\) is the stability condition of Chapter 6), so always \(C_P \geq C_V\): at constant pressure some of the heat goes into expansion work.
  • The two are equal when \(\alpha = 0\), for example water at 4 °C.
  • Check: for an ideal gas \(\alpha = 1/T\) and \(\kappa_T = 1/P\), giving \(TV\cdot\frac{1}{T^2}\cdot P = \frac{PV}{T} = nR\), the familiar \(C_P - C_V = nR\).
Proof

Treat \(S\) as \(S(T,V)\): \(dS = (\partial S/\partial T)_V\,dT + (\partial S/\partial V)_T\,dV\). Divide by \(dT\) at constant \(P\):

\[ \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V} + \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{\!T}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} \]

Multiply by \(T\): \(C_P = C_V + T(\partial S/\partial V)_T(\partial V/\partial T)_P\). Use the Maxwell relation \((\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V\), then the cyclic relation \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P/(\partial V/\partial P)_T = \alpha/\kappa_T\), and \((\partial V/\partial T)_P = V\alpha\):

\[ C_P - C_V = T\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\kappa_T}\cdot V\alpha = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} \]

Example · \(C_P - C_V\) for water

At 20 °C water has \(\alpha = 2.07\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), \(\kappa_T = 4.6\times10^{-10}\ \text{Pa}^{-1}\), molar volume \(V_m = 1.8\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3\) and \(C_P = 75.3\ \text{J/(mol·K)}\). Find the molar \(C_V\).

Show solution

\[ C_P - C_V = \frac{(293)(1.8\times10^{-5})(2.07\times10^{-4})^2}{4.6\times10^{-10}} \approx 0.49\ \text{J/(mol·K)} \]

So \(C_V \approx 74.8\ \text{J/(mol·K)}\). For a liquid the difference is under 1%, quite unlike an ideal gas (where it is \(R = 8.3\), about 30%): a liquid barely expands, so it does very little work.

5.6  Maxwell relations at work

The following results appear in homework and in later chapters. Each uses the same three steps: write the derivative you want → use a Maxwell relation to eliminate \(\partial S\) → substitute the equation of state.

(a) Entropy change of any substance

Two general entropy differentials

\[ dS = \frac{C_V}{T}\,dT + \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V}dV, \qquad dS = \frac{C_P}{T}\,dT - \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P}dP \]

Given an equation of state and a heat capacity, you can now find the entropy change of any substance between any two states: integrate along a convenient path (change \(T\) at fixed \(V\) or \(P\), then change \(V\) or \(P\) at fixed \(T\)). For an ideal gas this reproduces Chapter 4's \(\Delta S = nC_V\ln(T_2/T_1) + nR\ln(V_2/V_1)\).

Proof

The total differential of \(S(T,V)\) is \(dS = (\partial S/\partial T)_V dT + (\partial S/\partial V)_T dV\). The first coefficient is \(C_V/T\) by definition of heat capacity; the second becomes \((\partial P/\partial T)_V\) by the \(F\) relation. The second formula is the same argument with \(S(T,P)\) and the \(G\) relation.

(b) How internal energy depends on volume

Energy equation

\[ \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{\!T} = T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_{\!V} - P \]

This settles a question left open in Chapter 3. For an ideal gas, \(P = nRT/V\) gives \(T\cdot nR/V - P = 0\). So the ideal gas's \(U\) depends only on \(T\): it is a consequence, not an extra assumption.

Proof

Divide \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV\) by \(dV\) at constant \(T\): \((\partial U/\partial V)_T = T(\partial S/\partial V)_T - P\). Then use \((\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V\).

Example · A van der Waals gas cools in free expansion

A van der Waals gas, \(P = \frac{nRT}{V - nb} - \frac{an^2}{V^2}\) with constant \(C_V\), expands freely into a vacuum (no heat, no work) from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\). What happens to its temperature?

Show solution

\((\partial P/\partial T)_V = nR/(V - nb)\), so \((\partial U/\partial V)_T = \frac{nRT}{V-nb} - P = \frac{an^2}{V^2}\). Integrating, \(U = C_VT - \frac{an^2}{V} + \text{const}\).

Free expansion has \(W = 0\) and \(Q = 0\), so \(U\) is unchanged:

\[ C_V\Delta T = an^2\left(\frac{1}{V_2} - \frac{1}{V_1}\right) \lt 0 \]

The gas cools. As the molecules spread out they work against their mutual attraction (the \(a\) term), and that energy can only come from their thermal motion.

(c) Why adiabatic expansion cools

\[ \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_{\!S} = \frac{TV\alpha}{C_P} \gt 0 \quad(\text{when } \alpha \gt 0) \]

Lowering the pressure reversibly and adiabatically (isentropically) lowers the temperature. For an ideal gas, \(\alpha = 1/T\) gives \(dT/T = \frac{nR}{C_P}\,dP/P = \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\,dP/P\), which is the differential form of Chapter 3's adiabatic relation \(TP^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma} = \text{const}\).

Proof

By the cyclic relation, \((\partial T/\partial P)_S = -(\partial S/\partial P)_T/(\partial S/\partial T)_P\). The numerator is \(-V\alpha\) by the \(G\) relation; the denominator is \(C_P/T\).

(d) The Joule–Thomson (throttling) coefficient

\[ \mu_{JT} = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_{\!H} = \frac{V}{C_P}\,(T\alpha - 1) \]

For an ideal gas \(T\alpha = 1\), so throttling leaves the temperature unchanged. A real gas with \(T\alpha \gt 1\) cools on throttling (the principle behind gas liquefaction and refrigerators); with \(T\alpha \lt 1\) it warms. The boundary \(T\alpha = 1\) is the inversion curve.

Proof

\(\mu_{JT} = -(\partial H/\partial P)_T/(\partial H/\partial T)_P\). The denominator is \(C_P\). From \(dH = T\,dS + V\,dP\), \((\partial H/\partial P)_T = T(\partial S/\partial P)_T + V = -TV\alpha + V\). Substitute.

Extension: cooling by adiabatic demagnetisation (for interest)

For a magnetic system, replace \(-P\,dV\) by \(H\,dM\) (applied field \(H\), magnetisation \(M\)); the same steps give magnetic Maxwell relations. For a paramagnet obeying Curie's law \(M = aVH/T\):

\[ \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial H}\right)_{\!S} = \frac{aV}{C_H\,T}\,H \gt 0 \]

Reducing the field adiabatically lowers the temperature. This is a standard way to cool samples below 1 K, and the "isothermal step + adiabatic step" idea returns in Chapter 9 when we ask whether 0 K can be reached.

5.7  When particle number changes: the chemical potential

So far the particle number \(N\) has been fixed. But in a phase change (water molecules leaving the liquid for the vapour), a chemical reaction, or dissolving, the number of particles in each part changes. The fundamental relation then needs one more term:

Fundamental relation for open systems

\[ dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN, \qquad dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP + \mu\,dN \]

\(\mu\) is the chemical potential. Reading off coefficients:

\[ \mu = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{\!S,V} = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N}\right)_{\!T,P} \]

In words: how much \(G\) increases when one particle is added at fixed \(T\) and \(P\).

Why it exists · where it's used later

\(T\) decides which way heat flows (hot to cold). \(P\) decides which side expands (high to low pressure). \(\mu\) decides which way particles move: from high \(\mu\) to low \(\mu\) (proved in Chapter 6). So:

  • Chapter 7: two phases coexist ⟺ their chemical potentials are equal. Phase diagrams and the Clausius–Clapeyron equation follow from this single statement.
  • Chapter 8: chemical equilibrium, solutions and mixtures are all "set the \(\mu\)'s equal".

\(G = \mu N\): the chemical potential is the Gibbs energy per particle

\(G\) is extensive (doubling the system doubles \(G\)), but its natural variables \(T\) and \(P\) do not change with system size. So at fixed \(T, P\), \(G\) can only be proportional to \(N\), and the proportionality constant is \(\mu\):

Euler relation

\[ G = \mu N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu = \frac{G}{N} \equiv g(T,P) \]

For a single substance, \(\mu\) is just the Gibbs energy per molecule (or per mole). Dividing \(dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP\) by \(N\) gives the most important formula of Chapter 7:

\(d\mu\) for a single substance

\[ d\mu = -s\,dT + v\,dP \qquad (s = S/N,\ v = V/N) \]

Proof: the Euler and Gibbs–Duhem relations

Scale the system by a factor \(\lambda\): \(U(\lambda S, \lambda V, \lambda N) = \lambda U(S, V, N)\). Differentiate with respect to \(\lambda\) and set \(\lambda = 1\):

\[ U = TS - PV + \mu N \]

Hence \(G = U - TS + PV = \mu N\), and similarly \(H = TS + \mu N\), \(F = -PV + \mu N\).

Take the total differential of \(U = TS - PV + \mu N\) and subtract \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN\). What remains is the Gibbs–Duhem relation:

\[ S\,dT - V\,dP + N\,d\mu = 0 \]

It says \(T\), \(P\) and \(\mu\) cannot all vary independently: fix two and the third is determined. With several components, \(S\,dT - V\,dP + \sum_i N_i\,d\mu_i = 0\); this is the origin of the Gibbs phase rule in Chapter 7.

Chemical potential of an ideal gas

At constant temperature \(d\mu = v\,dP = \frac{k_BT}{P}\,dP\). Integrate from a reference pressure \(P_0\) to \(P\):

Ideal-gas chemical potential

\[ \mu(T,P) = \mu_0(T) + k_BT\ln\frac{P}{P_0} \qquad(\text{per mole: } RT\ln\tfrac{P}{P_0}) \]

Higher pressure means higher chemical potential: the molecules are more "eager" to leave. In a gas mixture, each component uses its own partial pressure \(P_i\). This formula is the starting point for chemical equilibrium constants in Chapter 8.

Chapter 6

Equilibrium and Stability

What this chapter does

Chapter 5 built \(F\) and \(G\). This chapter puts them to work by answering two questions about any system:

  • Where does it end up? At fixed \(T\) and \(P\) it slides downhill in \(G\) until \(G\) is as small as possible. Between two parts that can exchange things, equilibrium means equal \(T\), equal \(P\) and equal \(\mu\).
  • Does it stay there? Only if small disturbances die out, which requires \(C_V \gt 0\) and \(\kappa_T \gt 0\).
After this chapter you should be able to
  • say which potential is minimised for given constraints, and use the sign of \(\Delta G\) to decide which way a process goes;
  • state the three equilibrium conditions and the direction things flow when they are not met;
  • apply the stability conditions (e.g. constrain the exponents in \(S = aU^bV^cN^d\)) and use Le Chatelier's principle.

6.1  Which way does a process go?

For an isolated system the Second Law already answers this: entropy goes up, and equilibrium is maximum entropy. But a beaker on a lab bench is not isolated; it exchanges heat with the room. Tracking the room's entropy as well is tedious. What we want is a rule stated only in terms of the system. The potentials provide exactly that:

Direction of spontaneous change and equilibrium

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{fixed } T, V: && dF \le 0, &&\text{equilibrium at minimum } F \\ &\text{fixed } T, P: && dG \le 0, &&\text{equilibrium at minimum } G \end{aligned} \]

The constant-\(T\), constant-\(P\) case is by far the most common (anything open to the air at room temperature), so the working rule for the rest of the course is: a process at fixed \(T\) and \(P\) happens by itself only if it lowers \(G\).

Proof: where the minimum principles come from

Combine the Clausius inequality \(dS \geq \text{đ}Q/T\) with the First Law \(\text{đ}Q = dU - \text{đ}W\):

\[ T\,dS \geq dU - \text{đ}W \]

Fixed \(T\). Let \(\text{đ}W' = -\text{đ}W\) be the work done by the system. Then \(\text{đ}W' \le -dU + T\,dS = -d(U - TS) = -dF\), using \(T\,dS = d(TS)\) at constant \(T\). If no work is done (fixed \(V\)), \(dF \le 0\).

Fixed \(T\) and \(P\). The system must at least push back the surroundings, \(\text{đ}W' = P\,dV\) if that is its only work. Then \(P\,dV \le -dF\), and since \(P\) is constant, \(d(F + PV) = dG \le 0\).

Equality holds for reversible changes. At equilibrium nothing can lower the potential further, so it sits at a minimum: \(dF = 0, d^2F \gt 0\) or \(dG = 0, d^2G \gt 0\).

Example · Does ice melt at −10 °C? At +10 °C?

For melting ice, \(\Delta H = 6.01\ \text{kJ/mol}\), and at 0 °C the process is in equilibrium. Assuming \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta S\) don't change much with temperature, find the sign of \(\Delta G\) for melting at 263 K and at 283 K.

Show solution

At the melting point \(\Delta G = 0\), so \(\Delta S = \Delta H/T_m = 6010/273.15 = 22.0\ \text{J/(mol·K)}\). Then \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\):

\[ T = 263\ \text{K}: \ \Delta G = 6010 - 263(22.0) \approx +220\ \text{J/mol} \gt 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{ice does not melt} \]

\[ T = 283\ \text{K}: \ \Delta G = 6010 - 283(22.0) \approx -220\ \text{J/mol} \lt 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{ice melts} \]

Melting costs energy (\(\Delta H \gt 0\)) but gains entropy (\(\Delta S \gt 0\)). The \(-T\Delta S\) term grows with temperature, so above 0 °C entropy wins and below it energy wins. That tug of war inside \(G = H - TS\) is the reason phase transitions happen at a definite temperature.

Maximum useful work

The proof above also shows that at fixed \(T\) the work a system can deliver is at most \(-\Delta F\), and at fixed \(T\) and \(P\) the work beyond pushing back the atmosphere (e.g. electrical work) is at most \(-\Delta G\).

Example · A hydrogen fuel cell

For \(\mathrm{H_2} + \tfrac12\mathrm{O_2} \to \mathrm{H_2O(l)}\) at 298 K and 1 bar: \(\Delta H = -286\ \text{kJ/mol}\), \(\Delta G = -237\ \text{kJ/mol}\). How much electrical work can a fuel cell deliver per mole of water, and where does the rest of the energy go?

Show solution

Maximum non-expansion work \(= -\Delta G = 237\ \text{kJ}\). Burning the hydrogen would release \(-\Delta H = 286\ \text{kJ}\) of heat, but even a perfect fuel cell can turn only 237 kJ into electrical work. The remaining \(\Delta H - \Delta G = T\Delta S = -49\ \text{kJ}\) must be released as heat, because the products have lower entropy than the reactants (three molecules of gas become one of liquid), and that entropy has to be dumped into the surroundings.

6.2  Equilibrium between two parts: equal \(T\), \(P\), \(\mu\)

Now split a system into two parts that can exchange something. The rule is easy to state:

Equilibrium conditions

Whatever the two parts can exchange, the matching intensive variable must be equal on both sides:

\[ \text{energy} \Rightarrow T_1 = T_2, \qquad \text{volume} \Rightarrow P_1 = P_2, \qquad \text{particles} \Rightarrow \mu_1 = \mu_2 \]

When a condition is not met, the system moves to increase its total entropy, which fixes the direction of flow:

If the parts can exchangeEquilibrium needsOtherwise
Energy (heat)\(T_1 = T_2\)Heat flows from high \(T\) to low \(T\)
Volume (movable wall)\(P_1 = P_2\)The high-\(P\) side expands
Particles (permeable wall)\(\mu_1 = \mu_2\)Particles move from high \(\mu\) to low \(\mu\)

The first two rows are familiar. The third is the new one, and it is what Chapters 7 and 8 run on: whenever particles can move between two places (two phases, a solution and pure solvent, reactants and products), equilibrium means their chemical potentials are equal.

Proof: maximise the total entropy

Entropy is extensive, so \(S = S_1(U_1, V_1, N_1) + S_2(U_2, V_2, N_2)\). The whole system is isolated, so \(dU_2 = -dU_1\), \(dV_2 = -dV_1\), \(dN_2 = -dN_1\). From \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN\), the entropy derivatives are \(\partial S/\partial U = 1/T\), \(\partial S/\partial V = P/T\), \(\partial S/\partial N = -\mu/T\). So

\[ dS = \left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right)dU_1 + \left(\frac{P_1}{T_1} - \frac{P_2}{T_2}\right)dV_1 - \left(\frac{\mu_1}{T_1} - \frac{\mu_2}{T_2}\right)dN_1 \]

At equilibrium \(dS = 0\) for every allowed \(dU_1, dV_1, dN_1\), so each bracket vanishes: \(T_1 = T_2\), then \(P_1 = P_2\), then \(\mu_1 = \mu_2\).

Away from equilibrium a spontaneous change has \(dS \gt 0\). Take just the first term: if \(T_1 \lt T_2\) the bracket is positive, so \(dU_1 \gt 0\): energy flows into the colder part. The other two rows of the table follow the same way.

6.3  Stability: will it stay there?

"Minimum of \(G\)" (or maximum of \(S\)) contains two pieces of information. The first derivative being zero gives the equilibrium conditions above. The second derivative having the right sign says the state is a true minimum, not a maximum or a saddle, so that small random fluctuations get pushed back. That requirement constrains the material itself:

Stability conditions

\[ C_V \gt 0, \qquad \kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_{\!T} \gt 0 \]

Both conditions have a direct physical meaning:

  • \(C_V \gt 0\) (thermal stability). If a small region randomly gains some energy, its temperature rises, so heat flows back out of it. With a negative heat capacity, gaining energy would make it colder; it would pull in even more heat and the fluctuation would run away.
  • \(\kappa_T \gt 0\) (mechanical stability). If a small region gets squeezed, its pressure goes up and it pushes back. If squeezing lowered its pressure, it would keep collapsing.
Where it's used later
  • Chapter 7: below the critical temperature the van der Waals equation has a stretch with \((\partial P/\partial V)_T \gt 0\), i.e. \(\kappa_T \lt 0\). That uniform fluid is unstable and splits into liquid + gas; this is the liquid–gas transition.
  • Chapter 8: a mixture whose \(G(x)\) curves the wrong way is unstable in the same sense and separates into two phases (oil and water).
  • Chapter 5: \(\kappa_T \gt 0\) is why \(C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T \geq 0\).
Proof: the second variation of entropy

At fixed \(N\), let the energy and volume fluctuate by \(dU\), \(dV\). The second-order change of \(S(U,V)\) is

\[ d^2S = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[S_{UU}(dU)^2 + 2S_{UV}\,dU\,dV + S_{VV}(dV)^2\right] \]

The bracket equals \(d(S_U)\,dU + d(S_V)\,dV = d(1/T)\,dU + d(P/T)\,dV\). Now write everything in terms of \(dT\) and \(dV\), using \(d(1/T) = -dT/T^2\), \(dU = C_V\,dT + [T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P]\,dV\), \(d(P/T) = dP/T - P\,dT/T^2\) and \(dP = (\partial P/\partial T)_V dT + (\partial P/\partial V)_T dV\). Expanding, all cross terms \(dT\,dV\) cancel, leaving

\[ d^2S = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{C_V}{T^2}(dT)^2 + \frac{1}{2T}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_{\!T}(dV)^2 \]

For this to be negative for every \(dT\) and \(dV\), each coefficient must be negative: \(C_V \gt 0\) and \((\partial P/\partial V)_T \lt 0\), i.e. \(\kappa_T \gt 0\).

Example · Constraints on a model entropy (exam-style)

A substance has entropy \(S = a\,U^bV^cN^d\) with \(a \gt 0\). What constraints apply to \(b\), \(c\), \(d\)?

Show solution

Extensivity: doubling the system must double \(S\). \(S(\lambda U, \lambda V, \lambda N) = \lambda^{b+c+d}S\) must equal \(\lambda S\), so \(b + c + d = 1\).

Stability: \(S\) must curve downward in each variable:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial U^2} = ab(b-1)\,U^{b-2}V^cN^d \lt 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \lt b \lt 1 \]

and in the same way \(0 \lt c \lt 1\), \(0 \lt d \lt 1\). (The product \(x(x-1)\) is negative only for \(0 \lt x \lt 1\).)

Bonus check: \(1/T = \partial S/\partial U = ab\,U^{b-1}V^cN^d \gt 0\) needs \(b \gt 0\), consistent with the above.

Recipe for this type of question: (1) extensivity: \(S(\lambda U, \lambda V, \lambda N) = \lambda S\); (2) stability: every \(\partial^2 S/\partial X^2 \lt 0\); (3) sign checks: \(1/T = \partial S/\partial U \gt 0\), \(P/T = \partial S/\partial V \gt 0\).

6.4  Le Chatelier's principle

The stability conditions have a qualitative version that needs no calculation:

Le Chatelier's principle

When a system in equilibrium is disturbed (temperature, pressure, volume or concentration changed), it shifts to a new equilibrium in the direction that partly undoes the disturbance.

This is the same logic as \(C_V \gt 0\) and \(\kappa_T \gt 0\): a stable equilibrium pushes back. If it amplified the disturbance instead, it would not have been stable to begin with.

Example · Making ammonia

\(\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\) releases heat (\(\Delta h = -92\ \text{kJ/mol}\)). To get more ammonia, should you raise or lower the temperature? The pressure?

Show solution

Temperature: heating adds heat; the system responds by running in the direction that absorbs heat, which is backwards (the reverse reaction is endothermic). So lower temperature gives more NH₃ at equilibrium.

Pressure: the left side has 4 molecules of gas, the right only 2. Compressing raises the pressure; the system responds by shifting toward fewer gas molecules, i.e. forwards. So higher pressure gives more NH₃. Industrial plants run at very high pressure for this reason.

Chapter 8 turns both statements into equations (the law of mass action and the van 't Hoff equation).

How to apply it: identify what the disturbance adds (heat, pressure, a reactant) and ask which direction removes some of it. For temperature, look at the sign of \(\Delta h\): heating favours the endothermic direction. For pressure, count moles of gas: compression favours the side with fewer.

Chapter 7

Phase Equilibrium and Phase Transitions

What this chapter does

Ice melts at 0 °C and water boils at 100 °C, sharply, not gradually. Why at a definite temperature, and how does that temperature move with pressure? The whole chapter rests on two facts from Chapters 5–6:

  • at fixed \(T\) and \(P\), the phase with the lowest chemical potential \(\mu\) wins;
  • two phases can coexist only where their \(\mu\) are equal.
After this chapter you should be able to
  • explain a phase diagram (lines, triple point, critical point) and use the Gibbs phase rule;
  • use the Clausius–Clapeyron equation for slopes of phase boundaries, vapour pressures and triple points;
  • explain how the van der Waals fluid produces a liquid–gas transition (Maxwell construction, metastable states);
  • tell first-order from continuous transitions.

7.1  The key picture: lowest \(\mu\) wins

A phase is a region of matter that is uniform throughout: same density, composition and so on everywhere. Ice, liquid water and steam are three phases of one substance. (A uniform mix of He and N₂ gas is one phase; ice cubes in water are two.)

At given \(T\) and \(P\), each phase has its own chemical potential \(\mu(T,P)\), the Gibbs energy per molecule. Molecules drift from high \(\mu\) to low \(\mu\) (§6.2), so at any \((T,P)\) the substance ends up entirely in the phase with the lowest \(\mu\). Now recall from §5.7:

\[ \left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = -s, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial P}\right)_{\!T} = v \]

So on a plot of \(\mu\) against \(T\), each phase is a line sloping down with slope \(-s\). A gas has much more entropy per molecule than a liquid, which has more than a solid. So the gas line is the steepest and the solid line the flattest:

Read the picture from left to right:

  • At low \(T\) the solid has the lowest \(\mu\): it is stable.
  • The steeper liquid line overtakes it at \(T_m\): the substance melts.
  • The even steeper gas line overtakes the liquid at \(T_b\): it boils.

The transitions are sharp because the lowest line switches abruptly at a crossing. At the crossing itself the two \(\mu\) are equal and both phases can coexist:

Two phases coexist when

\[ T^{\text{I}} = T^{\text{II}}, \qquad P^{\text{I}} = P^{\text{II}}, \qquad \mu^{\text{I}}(T,P) = \mu^{\text{II}}(T,P) \]

The picture also explains pressure: raising \(P\) raises each \(\mu\) by \(v\,\Delta P\). The gas has by far the largest \(v\), so its line rises most and its crossing with the liquid moves to higher temperature. That is why water boils above 100 °C in a pressure cooker. §7.3 makes this quantitative.

Why a uniform phase can split in two (the entropy argument)

Suppose the entropy of a uniform substance, as a function of its energy, has a stretch where \(S(U)\) curves upward. Split the system into two halves with energies \(U + \Delta U\) and \(U - \Delta U\). The total energy is the same, but the average entropy is

\[ \tfrac{1}{2}\left[S(U + \Delta U) + S(U - \Delta U)\right] \gt S(U) \]

because a chord lies above an upward-curving curve. Splitting raises the entropy, so the uniform state is not the equilibrium state: the system separates into two parts with different energy densities, i.e. two phases. This is exactly the violation of the stability condition \(\partial^2S/\partial U^2 \lt 0\) from §6.3.

7.2  Phase diagrams and the Gibbs phase rule

Do the "lowest \(\mu\)" comparison at every \((T, P)\) and colour each point by the winning phase: that is a phase diagram. For water:

FeatureWhat it means
RegionsOne phase is stable: ice, water or vapour. \(T\) and \(P\) can both be varied freely.
LinesTwo phases coexist (melting, boiling, sublimation curves). Along a line, choosing \(T\) fixes \(P\). For example, at 100 °C water and steam coexist only at 1 atm.
Triple pointAll three lines meet and all three phases coexist: \(273.16\ \text{K}\) (0.01 °C), \(611.66\ \text{Pa}\) for water. This is the reference point of the temperature scale (Chapter 1).
Critical pointThe liquid–gas line ends (water: about 374 °C, 22 MPa). Beyond it, liquid and gas stop being distinct phases.

Why are coexistence regions lines and points rather than areas? Counting answers it. With \(K\) components and \(P\) coexisting phases, the number of intensive variables you can still change freely is

Gibbs phase rule

\[ f = K + 2 - P \]

For a pure substance (\(K = 1\)): one phase gives \(f = 2\) (an area), two phases \(f = 1\) (a line), three phases \(f = 0\) (a single point). Four phases of a pure substance can never coexist.

Proof: count unknowns and equations

Each phase is described by \(T\), \(P\) and its composition. Composition is \(K\) mole fractions that add to 1, so \(K - 1\) independent ones. Each phase therefore has \(K + 1\) intensive variables, and \(P\) phases have \(P(K+1)\).

Equilibrium requires \(T\), \(P\) and each of the \(K\) chemical potentials to be equal across all \(P\) phases. Each "equal across \(P\) phases" is \(P - 1\) equations, and there are \(K + 2\) such quantities: \((P-1)(K+2)\) equations.

Free variables \(= P(K+1) - (P-1)(K+2) = K + 2 - P\).

7.3  The Clausius–Clapeyron equation

A coexistence line has a definite slope, and we can predict it from two measurable numbers: the latent heat and the volume change.

Clausius–Clapeyron equation

\[ \frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta v} \]

Here \(L = T\Delta s\) is the latent heat (heat absorbed going from phase I to phase II) and \(\Delta v\) the change in volume, both for the same amount of substance (per molecule, per mole, or per kilogram).

What it tells you
  • Boiling: \(L \gt 0\) and the gas takes up far more volume (\(\Delta v \gt 0\)), so \(dP/dT \gt 0\): higher pressure → higher boiling point.
  • Melting of ice: \(L \gt 0\), but liquid water is denser than ice, so \(\Delta v \lt 0\) and \(dP/dT \lt 0\). Squeezing ice lowers its melting point. Almost every other substance behaves the opposite way.
Proof (three lines)

Along the coexistence line, \(\mu^{\text{I}} = \mu^{\text{II}}\) at every point, so moving along the line their changes are equal: \(d\mu^{\text{I}} = d\mu^{\text{II}}\). With \(d\mu = -s\,dT + v\,dP\):

\[ -s^{\text{I}}\,dT + v^{\text{I}}\,dP = -s^{\text{II}}\,dT + v^{\text{II}}\,dP \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{s^{\text{II}} - s^{\text{I}}}{v^{\text{II}} - v^{\text{I}}} \]

The phase change happens at constant \(T\) and is reversible, so the heat absorbed is \(L = T(s^{\text{II}} - s^{\text{I}})\).

Vapour pressure: the integrated form

For liquid → gas (or solid → gas), three approximations make the equation easy to integrate: the gas volume dominates (\(\Delta V \approx V_g\)); the vapour is ideal (\(V_g = RT/P\) per mole); and \(L\) is roughly constant. Then \(dP/dT = LP/(RT^2)\), which integrates to

Vapour-pressure curve

\[ \ln P = -\frac{L}{RT} + \text{const}, \qquad \ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = -\frac{L}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right) \]

(\(L\) per mole.) Plotting \(\ln P\) against \(1/T\) gives a straight line of slope \(-L/R\); reading \(L\) off such a slope is a standard exam step.

Example 1 · Pressure cooker

A pressure cooker runs at 2 atm. At what temperature does the water boil? (\(L_{\text{vap}} = 40.7\ \text{kJ/mol}\); at 1 atm it boils at 373.15 K.)

Show solution

\[ \frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{R}{L}\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{1}{373.15} - \frac{8.314\,\ln 2}{40\,700} = 2.680\times10^{-3} - 1.42\times10^{-4} \]

so \(T_2 \approx 394\ \text{K} \approx 121\ \text{°C}\). (The measured value is about 120 °C.) Food cooks much faster at 121 °C than at 100 °C.

Example 2 · How much pressure to melt ice?

Ice: \(L_{\text{fus}} = 6.01\ \text{kJ/mol}\); molar volumes \(19.65\ \text{cm}^3\) (ice) and \(18.02\ \text{cm}^3\) (water). By how much must the pressure rise to lower the melting point by 1 K?

Show solution

\(\Delta v = 18.02 - 19.65 = -1.63\ \text{cm}^3/\text{mol} = -1.63\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3/\text{mol}\).

\[ \frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{6010}{(273.15)(-1.63\times10^{-6})} \approx -1.35\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa/K} \approx -133\ \text{atm/K} \]

About 133 atm per kelvin: the effect exists but is small, because \(\Delta v\) for melting is tiny compared with boiling.

Example 3 · Triple point of ammonia (course example)

The vapour pressures (in Pa) of liquid and solid ammonia are \(\ln P = 24.38 - 3063/T\) (liquid) and \(\ln P = 27.92 - 3754/T\) (solid). Find (i) the triple point, (ii) the latent heats of vaporisation, sublimation and fusion.

Show solution

Latent heats. Compare each line with \(\ln P = -L/(RT) + \text{const}\): the coefficient of \(1/T\) is \(L/R\).

\[ L_{\text{vap}} = 3063 \times 8.31 \approx 2.55\times10^{4}\ \text{J/mol}, \qquad L_{\text{sub}} = 3754 \times 8.31 \approx 3.12\times10^{4}\ \text{J/mol} \]

Triple point. There, liquid and solid have the same vapour pressure. Set the two lines equal:

\[ 24.38 - \frac{3063}{T_t} = 27.92 - \frac{3754}{T_t} \;\Rightarrow\; T_t = \frac{691}{3.54} \approx 195.2\ \text{K}, \qquad \ln P_t = 24.38 - \frac{3063}{195.2} \;\Rightarrow\; P_t \approx 5.93\ \text{kPa} \]

Fusion. Going solid → gas directly or via the liquid must cost the same (entropy is a state function), so \(L_{\text{sub}} = L_{\text{fus}} + L_{\text{vap}}\) and \(L_{\text{fus}} \approx 5.7\times10^{3}\ \text{J/mol}\).

Note: the slide prints the solid constant as 27.29, which would put the triple point near 237 K. The value 27.92 reproduces the slide's own answer of 195.2 K and 5934 Pa.

Example 4 · Diamond vs graphite (course example)

At 298 K and 1 bar graphite is stable: its molar \(G\) is 2900 J/mol lower than diamond's. Molar volumes: diamond \(3.42\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\), graphite \(5.31\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\). (i) Why does high pressure favour diamond, and roughly what pressure is needed? (ii) At 500 K the molar entropies are 7.59 J/K (diamond) and 12.33 J/K (graphite). Find the slope of the boundary.

Show solution

(i) \((\partial G/\partial P)_T = V\): raising the pressure increases both \(G\)'s, but graphite's faster because its volume is larger. The lines cross when the 2900 J/mol gap is used up:

\[ \Delta P \approx \frac{2900}{(5.31 - 3.42)\times10^{-6}} \approx 1.5\times10^{9}\ \text{Pa} \approx 15\ \text{kbar} \]

(ii) Clausius–Clapeyron with \(\Delta\) = graphite − diamond:

\[ \frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V} = \frac{12.33 - 7.59}{1.89\times10^{-6}} \approx 2.5\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa/K} \approx 25\ \text{bar/K} \]

Positive: graphite has more entropy, so heating favours it, and each extra kelvin needs about 25 bar more to keep diamond stable. (The 500 K entropies come from the 298 K table values plus \(\int C_P\,dT/T\); see the full notes.)

Note: the slide swaps the two molar volumes. Diamond is the denser phase, and the slide's own calculation uses \(V_{\text{graphite}} - V_{\text{diamond}} = (5.31 - 3.42)\times10^{-6}\).

7.4  The van der Waals fluid: where the liquid–gas transition comes from

So far we have taken the existence of liquid and gas for granted. The van der Waals equation shows how a single equation of state produces both. Per molecule (\(v = V/N\)):

\[ P = \frac{k_BT}{v - b} - \frac{a}{v^2} \]

\(b\) accounts for the finite size of molecules, \(a\) for their mutual attraction. At high temperature the isotherms look like an ideal gas. Below a critical temperature \(T_c\) each isotherm develops a wiggle containing a stretch where \((\partial P/\partial V)_T \gt 0\), i.e. \(\kappa_T \lt 0\). By §6.3 that is unstable: a uniform fluid there splits into two phases, liquid and gas.

Van der Waals critical point

\[ v_c = 3b, \qquad k_BT_c = \frac{8a}{27b}, \qquad P_c = \frac{a}{27b^2}, \qquad \frac{P_cv_c}{k_BT_c} = \frac{3}{8} \]

At \(T_c\) the wiggle shrinks to a single point where the isotherm is momentarily flat and has an inflection; above \(T_c\) liquid and gas can no longer be told apart.

Proof: critical constants

Require \((\partial P/\partial v)_T = 0\) and \((\partial^2 P/\partial v^2)_T = 0\):

\[ -\frac{k_BT}{(v-b)^2} + \frac{2a}{v^3} = 0, \qquad \frac{2k_BT}{(v-b)^3} - \frac{6a}{v^4} = 0 \]

The first gives \(k_BT = 2a(v-b)^2/v^3\). Substituting into the second gives \(4/(v-b) = 6/v\), so \(v = 3b\). Then \(k_BT_c = 2a(2b)^2/(3b)^3 = 8a/27b\) and \(P_c = k_BT_c/(2b) - a/(9b^2) = a/27b^2\).

The Maxwell equal-area construction

The real isotherm doesn't follow the wiggle. It runs flat at the coexistence pressure, where liquid and gas coexist. Which flat line? The one that cuts off equal areas above and below:

Why equal areas

Points 2 (gas) and 6 (liquid) coexist, so \(\mu_2 = \mu_6\), i.e. \(G_2 = G_6\). Going from 2 to 6 along the wiggle 2→3→4→5→6 at fixed \(T\), \(dG = V\,dP\), so

\[ G_6 - G_2 = \int_{\text{loop}} V\,dP = 0 \]

On the \(P\)–\(V\) diagram this integral is the area between the curve and the flat line, with the part above the line and the part below counting with opposite signs. Zero total means area A = area B.

Equivalently, the Gibbs energy along the isotherm (from \(dG = V\,dP\)) is \(G = -Nk_BT\ln(V - Nb) + \frac{(Nk_BT)(Nb)}{V - Nb} - \frac{2aN^2}{V} + c(T)\). Plotted against \(P\) it forms a loop whose two stable branches cross exactly at \(P_{\text{coex}}\).

Metastable states: superheating and supercooling

Not every point on the wiggle is equally forbidden:

Stretch of the curve\(\kappa_T\)StatusReal example
2 → 3 (gas, past coexistence)\(\gt 0\)MetastableSupersaturated vapour (e.g. in a cloud chamber)
3 → 4 → 5\(\lt 0\)Unstable: cannot exist—
5 → 6 (liquid, past coexistence)\(\gt 0\)MetastableSuperheated water above 100 °C without boiling

A metastable state is a local, not global, minimum of \(G\): small fluctuations are pushed back (because \(\kappa_T \gt 0\)), so it can last a long time, but a big enough kick (a dust particle, a scratch, a seed crystal) tips it into the stable phase. Supercooled water below 0 °C is the same idea for freezing.

Binodal (coexistence) curve
Joins the coexistence points (2 and 6) at every \(T\); found by the Maxwell construction.
Spinodal curve
Joins the turning points (3 and 5) where \((\partial P/\partial V)_T = 0\). Inside it the fluid is unstable.

Metastable states lie between the two curves. Both curves meet at the critical point.

7.5  First-order and continuous transitions

In melting and boiling, \(\mu\) is continuous across the transition (the two lines cross), but its slopes jump: \(\partial\mu/\partial T = -s\) and \(\partial\mu/\partial P = v\) are different for the two phases. So entropy and volume change abruptly, and there is a latent heat \(L = T\Delta s\). Because the first derivatives jump, these are called first-order transitions.

In some transitions the two phases become identical at the transition point: \(\Delta s = 0\), \(\Delta v = 0\), no latent heat. Only second derivatives of \(\mu\) (heat capacity, compressibility, expansion coefficient) jump or blow up. These are continuous (second-order) transitions: the liquid–gas transition exactly at the critical point, a magnet losing its magnetisation, a metal becoming superconducting. Their theory is beyond this course.

Chapter 8

Applications: Reactions, Solutions and Mixtures

What this chapter does

This chapter looks like a list of unrelated topics: chemical equilibrium, Raoult's law, boiling and freezing points, osmosis, mixing and separating. In fact every one of them is solved by the same three steps:

  1. Write the chemical potential \(\mu\) of each species in each place it can be.
  2. Set the \(\mu\)'s equal wherever particles can move (Chapter 6).
  3. If the effect is small, expand to first order using \((\partial\mu/\partial P)_T = v\) and \((\partial\mu/\partial T)_P = -s\).

The only new physics is how mixing changes \(\mu\), and that comes from the entropy of mixing (§8.1).

After this chapter you should be able to
  • write the law of mass action for a reaction and use the van 't Hoff equation;
  • derive and use Raoult's law, the boiling/freezing-point shifts and the osmotic pressure;
  • explain when two substances mix or separate, and use the common-tangent and lever rules on a phase diagram.

8.1  The entropy of mixing

Put gas A in the left half of a box and gas B in the right half, at the same \(T\) and \(P\). Remove the partition. The gases mix and never unmix by themselves: mixing is irreversible, so it must create entropy. For ideal gases nothing else changes (no interactions, so \(\Delta U = 0\) and \(T\) stays the same); each gas simply spreads into a larger volume, like a free expansion.

Entropy of mixing (ideal)

\[ \Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -Nk_B\sum_i x_i\ln x_i \;\gt\; 0, \qquad x_i = \frac{N_i}{N} \]

Since every \(x_i\) is between 0 and 1, every \(\ln x_i \lt 0\) and the result is positive. Equal amounts of two gases (\(x = \tfrac12\)) give \(Nk_B\ln 2\), where \(N\) is the total number of molecules.

Why this matters for the rest of the chapter

Mixing adds \(-T\Delta S_{\text{mix}}\) to \(G\), which lowers it. Differentiating that term gives each component a chemical potential lowered by a \(k_BT\ln x\) term:

\[ \mu_i = \mu_i^{0} + k_BT\ln x_i \]

where \(\mu_i^0\) is the pure substance's value. This single fact drives everything below: a solvent with something dissolved in it has a lower \(\mu\) than the pure solvent, so water "prefers" to be in the solution.

Example · Mixing gases

(a) Find \(\Delta S\) when 1 mol of N₂ and 1 mol of O₂ (same \(T\), \(P\)) mix. (b) Find the entropy of mixing per mole of air (79% N₂, 21% O₂).

Show solution

(a) \(N = 2\) mol, \(x = \tfrac12\) each: \(\Delta S = -2R(\tfrac12\ln\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\ln\tfrac12) = 2R\ln 2 \approx 11.5\ \text{J/K}\).

(b) \(\Delta S = -R(0.79\ln 0.79 + 0.21\ln 0.21) = 8.314 \times 0.514 \approx 4.27\ \text{J/(mol·K)}\).

Proof of the formula, and the Gibbs paradox

Let species \(i\) start with \(N_i\) molecules in its own compartment \(V_i\), all at the same density, so \(V_i/V = x_i\). An ideal gas's entropy depends on volume through \(Nk_B\ln V\). Before mixing: \(k_B\sum_i N_i\ln V_i = k_BN\sum_i x_i\ln x_i + k_BN\ln V\). After mixing every species fills \(V\): \(k_BN\ln V\). The difference is \(-Nk_B\sum_i x_i\ln x_i\).

Gibbs paradox. Repeat with the same gas on both sides. The formula would still predict \(2Nk_B\ln 2\), yet nothing physical has happened; reinserting the partition restores the original state. The resolution is that identical molecules are indistinguishable: the correct volume dependence is \(Nk_B\ln(V/N)\) (as in the Sackur–Tetrode equation), and joining two identical gases at the same density leaves \(V/N\) unchanged, so \(\Delta S = 0\). For different gases, each species' own \(V/N_i\) still grows, so the mixing entropy survives.

8.2  Chemical equilibrium: the law of mass action

Take \(\mathrm{N_2} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{NH_3}\) at fixed \(T\) and \(P\). If the reaction advances a little, N₂ drops by \(\varepsilon\), H₂ by \(3\varepsilon\), and NH₃ rises by \(2\varepsilon\). At equilibrium \(G\) is a minimum, so \(dG = \sum\mu_i\,dN_i = 0\) for this change:

Condition for chemical equilibrium

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{N_2}} + 3\,\mu_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 2\,\mu_{\mathrm{NH_3}} \]

The chemical potentials balance with the same coefficients as the reaction equation. Now substitute the ideal-gas form \(\mu_i = g_i^0 + RT\ln(P_i/P_0)\) per mole (§5.7), with \(P_i\) the partial pressure:

Law of mass action

\[ \frac{(P_{\mathrm{NH_3}}/P_0)^2}{(P_{\mathrm{N_2}}/P_0)(P_{\mathrm{H_2}}/P_0)^3} = K(T), \qquad K(T) = e^{-\Delta g_0/RT} \]

\(\Delta g_0 = 2g^0_{\mathrm{NH_3}} - g^0_{\mathrm{N_2}} - 3g^0_{\mathrm{H_2}}\) (products minus reactants, at the reference pressure \(P_0\)). The equilibrium constant \(K\) depends only on temperature: whatever amounts you start with, the partial pressures adjust until this ratio equals \(K(T)\). Large \(K\) (negative \(\Delta g_0\)) means mostly products.

Proof: from equal \(\mu\)'s to \(K\)

\[ g^0_{\mathrm{N_2}} + RT\ln\tfrac{P_{\mathrm{N_2}}}{P_0} + 3g^0_{\mathrm{H_2}} + 3RT\ln\tfrac{P_{\mathrm{H_2}}}{P_0} = 2g^0_{\mathrm{NH_3}} + 2RT\ln\tfrac{P_{\mathrm{NH_3}}}{P_0} \]

Move the \(g^0\) terms to one side, combine the logarithms, and divide by \(RT\): \(\ln[\ldots] = -\Delta g_0/RT\). Exponentiate.

Pressure effect (Le Chatelier, quantitatively). With mole fractions \(x_i\) and total pressure \(P\), \(P_i = x_iP\), so \(\frac{x_{\mathrm{NH_3}}^2}{x_{\mathrm{N_2}}x_{\mathrm{H_2}}^3} = K(T)\,(P/P_0)^2\). The power 2 is (gas moles on the left) − (gas moles on the right). Raising \(P\) increases the fraction of NH₃, as §6.4 predicted.

How \(K\) changes with temperature

van 't Hoff equation

\[ \frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta h_0}{RT^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{K_2}{K_1} = -\frac{\Delta h_0}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right) \]

\(\Delta h_0\) is the heat absorbed by the reaction. Endothermic (\(\Delta h_0 \gt 0\)): \(K\) grows with \(T\). Exothermic (\(\Delta h_0 \lt 0\)): \(K\) shrinks with \(T\). This is the quantitative form of Le Chatelier. Notice it has exactly the same shape as the vapour-pressure law \(d\ln P/dT = L/RT^2\) from §7.3, because evaporation is just the "reaction" liquid ⇌ vapour.

Proof

\(\frac{d}{dT}\left(-\frac{\Delta g_0}{RT}\right) = -\frac{1}{R}\,\frac{T\,(d\Delta g_0/dT) - \Delta g_0}{T^2}\). Use \(d\Delta g_0/dT = -\Delta s_0\) and \(\Delta g_0 = \Delta h_0 - T\Delta s_0\): the numerator becomes \(-T\Delta s_0 - \Delta h_0 + T\Delta s_0 = -\Delta h_0\).

Example · Ammonia at high temperature

For \(\mathrm{N_2} + 3\mathrm{H_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\Delta h_0 = -92\ \text{kJ}\). Treating \(\Delta h_0\) as constant, by what factor does \(K\) change between 298 K and 700 K?

Show solution

\[ \ln\frac{K_{700}}{K_{298}} = -\frac{-92\,000}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{700} - \frac{1}{298}\right) = 11\,066 \times (-1.927\times10^{-3}) \approx -21.3 \]

so \(K\) drops by a factor of about \(e^{21} \approx 2\times10^{9}\). Heating strongly disfavours ammonia. Industry still runs hot because the reaction is far too slow at room temperature, and compensates with very high pressure (the \((P/P_0)^2\) factor above).

8.3  Dilute solutions

A dilute solution has a little solute B (salt, sugar) dissolved in a lot of solvent A (water): \(N_B \ll N_A\). Solute molecules are far apart, so they interact only with the solvent, never with each other. Write \(x = N_B/N_A\) for the (small) solute fraction. The one result we need is how dissolving the solute changes the solvent's chemical potential:

Solvent and solute in a dilute solution

\[ \mu_A = \mu_0(T,P) - x\,k_BT, \qquad \mu_B = f(T,P) + k_BT\ln x \]

\(\mu_0\) is the chemical potential of the pure solvent; \(f\) describes solute–solvent interactions and never appears in the results. The first formula is the important one: dissolving anything lowers the solvent's \(\mu\) by \(xk_BT\), regardless of what the solute is. It is the \(k_BT\ln x_A\) term from §8.1 with \(\ln(1-x) \approx -x\).

Why this matters

Because only the number of dissolved particles enters (not their identity), the effects in §8.4 are called colligative properties. One mole of sugar and one mole of urea shift the freezing point of water by the same amount. A salt like NaCl splits into two ions, so it counts double.

Proof: the Gibbs energy of a dilute solution

Start from pure solvent, \(G = N_A\mu_0\). Adding \(N_B\) solute molecules adds an interaction term \(N_Bf(T,P)\) (proportional to \(N_B\), since solute molecules don't meet each other) and the mixing term \(-T\Delta S_{\text{mix}} = k_BT[N_B\ln(N_B/N) + N_A\ln(N_A/N)]\). For \(N_B \ll N_A\), \(N \approx N_A\) and \(N_A\ln(N_A/N) \approx -N_B\), so

\[ G \approx N_A\mu_0 + N_Bf - N_Bk_BT\ln N_A + N_Bk_BT\ln N_B - N_Bk_BT \]

Differentiating: \(\mu_A = \partial G/\partial N_A = \mu_0 - N_Bk_BT/N_A\) and \(\mu_B = \partial G/\partial N_B = f + k_BT\ln(N_B/N_A)\).

8.4  Colligative properties

All four results below come from the three-step recipe in the chapter introduction. The pure solvent's own equilibrium point (its boiling point, freezing point, or vapour pressure) is the point we expand around.

Raoult's law: vapour pressure is lowered

Raoult's law

\[ P \approx P_0(1 - x) \]

\(P_0\) is the vapour pressure of the pure solvent. With solute dissolved, the solvent's \(\mu\) in the liquid is lower, so fewer molecules "want" to escape and the equilibrium vapour pressure drops by the fraction \(x\).

Proof

Step 2: the vapour is pure solvent, so \(\mu_{\text{gas}}(T,P) = \mu_0(T,P) - xk_BT\). For pure solvent, \(\mu_{\text{gas}}(T,P_0) = \mu_0(T,P_0)\).

Step 3: expand both sides about \(P_0\) using \(\partial\mu/\partial P = v\): \(v_{\text{gas}}(P - P_0) = v_{\text{liq}}(P - P_0) - xk_BT\). Drop \(v_{\text{liq}} \ll v_{\text{gas}}\) and use \(v_{\text{gas}} = k_BT/P_0\): \(P - P_0 = -xP_0\).

Boiling point goes up, freezing point goes down

Boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression

\[ \Delta T_b \approx +\frac{x\,RT_b^2}{L_{\text{vap}}}, \qquad \Delta T_m \approx -\frac{x\,RT_m^2}{L_{\text{fus}}} \]

(\(L\) per mole; per molecule replace \(R\) by \(k_B\).) The solute stays in the liquid; the vapour and the ice are pure solvent. Dissolving lowers \(\mu\) of the liquid only. In the \(\mu(T)\) picture of §7.1, the liquid line moves down, so it crosses the gas line later (higher \(T_b\)) and the solid line earlier (lower \(T_m\)). The liquid range widens at both ends. This is why salted roads don't ice over and why antifreeze is added to car radiators.

Proof (boiling; freezing is identical)

At fixed \(P\), the solution boils where \(\mu_{\text{gas}}(T) = \mu_0(T) - xk_BT\). Pure solvent: \(\mu_{\text{gas}}(T_b) = \mu_0(T_b)\). Expand about \(T_b\) with \(\partial\mu/\partial T = -s\):

\[ -s_{\text{gas}}\,\Delta T = -s_{\text{liq}}\,\Delta T - xk_BT \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta T = \frac{xk_BT_b}{s_{\text{gas}} - s_{\text{liq}}} = \frac{xk_BT_b^2}{L_{\text{vap}}} \]

For freezing, replace the gas by ice: \((s_{\text{ice}} - s_{\text{liq}})\Delta T = xk_BT\), and since \(s_{\text{ice}} \lt s_{\text{liq}}\) the shift is negative: \(\Delta T = -xk_BT_m^2/L_{\text{fus}}\).

Osmotic pressure

A semi-permeable membrane lets water through but not the solute. Put pure water on one side and solution on the other. Water flows into the solution (its \(\mu\) is lower there) until the solution side is at a higher pressure that stops the flow, e.g. a column of height \(h\) with \(\Pi = \rho gh\).

Osmotic pressure

\[ \Pi = P_2 - P_1 = \frac{N_Bk_BT}{V} = \frac{n_BRT}{V} \]

It has exactly the form of the ideal gas law: the dissolved particles push as if they were a gas filling the solution's volume.

Proof

Water's \(\mu\) must match across the membrane: \(\mu_0(T,P_1) = \mu_0(T,P_2) - xk_BT\). Expand \(\mu_0(T,P_2) \approx \mu_0(T,P_1) + v_w(P_2 - P_1)\) with \(v_w\) the volume per water molecule: \(v_w(P_2 - P_1) = xk_BT = N_Bk_BT/N_A\). Since \(N_Av_w \approx V\), \(P_2 - P_1 = N_Bk_BT/V\).

Example · Seawater

Seawater has about 35 g of NaCl per kg. Estimate (a) how much its vapour pressure is lowered, (b) its boiling and freezing points, (c) the pressure a desalination plant must exceed to push fresh water out through a membrane. (\(L_{\text{vap}} = 40.7\), \(L_{\text{fus}} = 6.01\ \text{kJ/mol}\).)

Show solution

35 g NaCl = 0.60 mol, which gives 1.20 mol of ions. 965 g of water = 53.6 mol. So \(x \approx 1.20/54.8 \approx 0.022\).

(a) Raoult: the vapour pressure is about 2.2% below that of pure water.

(b) \(\Delta T_b \approx 0.022 \times 8.314 \times 373^2/40\,700 \approx +0.6\ \text{K}\); \(\Delta T_m \approx -0.022 \times 8.314 \times 273^2/6010 \approx -2.3\ \text{K}\). (Measured: seawater freezes near −1.9 °C. The dilute-solution formulas slightly overestimate at this concentration.)

(c) About 1200 mol of ions per m³: \(\Pi \approx 1200 \times 8.314 \times 298 \approx 3\times10^6\ \text{Pa} \approx 30\ \text{atm}\). Reverse-osmosis plants must apply more than this.

8.5  Binary mixtures: mix or separate?

Now drop "dilute": mix two components A and B in any proportion, \(x = N_B/(N_A + N_B)\) from 0 (pure A) to 1 (pure B). Will they form one uniform mixture, or separate like oil and water? Compare Gibbs energies.

Unmixed, \(G\) is just a weighted average of the pure substances — a straight line in \(x\). Mixing ideal components adds only the mixing entropy:

Ideal mixture

\[ G = (1-x)G_A^0 + xG_B^0 + Nk_BT\left[(1-x)\ln(1-x) + x\ln x\right] \]

The bracket is negative and curves upward, so \(G(x)\) sags below the straight line everywhere: ideal components always mix, in any proportion. (In fact the slope of \(x\ln x\) is infinite at \(x \to 0\), so adding the first trace of B always lowers \(G\): no substance in contact with another stays perfectly pure.)

When mixing costs energy

In real mixtures, unlike molecules may attract each other less than like ones (oil and water), so mixing raises the energy. Now two terms compete: the energy term makes \(G(x)\) bulge upward in the middle, while the entropy term pulls it down. At low temperature the energy wins and \(G(x)\) develops a stretch that curves the wrong way. A uniform mixture there is unstable and lowers its \(G\) by splitting into an A-rich phase and a B-rich phase.

Compositions between \(x_a\) and \(x_b\) are a solubility gap: the two components are immiscible there. Raising \(T\) strengthens the entropy term, the gap narrows, and above a certain temperature it disappears. Plotting \(x_a\) and \(x_b\) against \(T\) gives a dome, with two phases inside and one uniform mixture outside, just like the liquid–gas dome in §7.4.

8.6  The common-tangent rule and the lever rule

Two practical tools for reading binary phase diagrams:

Common-tangent rule

Two phases \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) coexist at the compositions where a single straight line is tangent to both \(G(x)\) curves (or both parts of one curve).

Why: equilibrium needs \(\mu_A\) equal in both phases and \(\mu_B\) equal in both phases. The tangent line to \(g(x) = G/N\) at composition \(x\) hits the \(x = 0\) axis at \(\mu_A\) and the \(x = 1\) axis at \(\mu_B\). Equal \(\mu\)'s in two phases means equal intercepts, which means the same tangent line.

Proof: chemical potentials are tangent intercepts

With \(G = Ng(x)\), \(N = N_A + N_B\), \(x = N_B/N\): \(\partial x/\partial N_A = -x/N\), \(\partial x/\partial N_B = (1-x)/N\). So

\[ \mu_A = \frac{\partial G}{\partial N_A} = g - x\,g'(x), \qquad \mu_B = \frac{\partial G}{\partial N_B} = g + (1-x)\,g'(x) \]

The tangent at \(x\) is \(\ell(y) = g(x) + g'(x)(y - x)\); \(\ell(0) = \mu_A\) and \(\ell(1) = \mu_B\).

Once the coexisting compositions \(x^\alpha\) and \(x^\beta\) are known, conservation of B fixes how much of each phase there is. If a fraction \(\mathcal{P}\) of the particles is in phase \(\beta\) and the overall composition is \(x_0\), then \((1-\mathcal{P})x^\alpha + \mathcal{P}x^\beta = x_0\):

Lever rule

\[ \mathcal{P}_\beta = \frac{x_0 - x^\alpha}{x^\beta - x^\alpha}, \qquad \mathcal{P}_\alpha = \frac{x^\beta - x_0}{x^\beta - x^\alpha} \]

On the diagram: the amount of each phase is proportional to the length of the segment on the opposite side of \(x_0\). If \(x_0\) is close to \(x^\alpha\), most of the system is phase \(\alpha\).

Building a phase diagram

Given \(G(x)\) curves for, say, the liquid and gas at many temperatures: wherever the curves cross, draw the common tangent and mark its two touching points; then plot those compositions against \(T\). For two liquids with boiling points \(T_A \lt T_B\) this gives a lens-shaped two-phase region between \(T_A\) and \(T_B\). The gas is always richer in the more volatile component (the lower boiling point).

Example · Liquefying air (course example)

Air (21% O₂, 79% N₂) is cooled to 80.5 K. The phase diagram shows gas with oxygen fraction \(x_g = 0.15\) coexisting with liquid at \(x_l = 0.40\). What fraction of the molecules is still gas?

Show solution

Lever rule with \(x_0 = 0.21\):

\[ \mathcal{P}_{\text{gas}} = \frac{x_l - x_0}{x_l - x_g} = \frac{0.40 - 0.21}{0.40 - 0.15} = 0.76 \]

76% is still gas; 24% has condensed. The liquid is oxygen-rich because oxygen boils at a higher temperature (90 K) than nitrogen (77 K). (The slide labels this temperature range "°C"; it is kelvin.)

Eutectic mixtures

Some pairs mix completely as liquids but form two different solid phases: \(\alpha\) (mostly A) and \(\beta\) (mostly B). On cooling, solid \(\alpha\) or \(\beta\) starts to separate out, and the melting curves fall from the pure melting points \(T_A\) and \(T_B\) towards each other. They meet at the eutectic point, the composition with the lowest melting temperature of all, lower than either pure substance. This is freezing-point depression pushed to large concentrations. Example from the slides: water + ethylene glycol (car coolant) has its eutectic near −49 °C at about 56% glycol; a 50% mixture freezes at about −31 °C.

Chapter 9

The Third Law of Thermodynamics

What this chapter does

The First and Second Laws only ever talk about changes, \(\Delta U\) and \(\Delta S\). The Third Law is about the bottom of the temperature scale: it gives entropy an absolute zero, and it explains why 0 K can be approached but never reached. It is a short chapter that reuses tools from Chapters 4, 5 and 7.

After this chapter you should be able to
  • state the Third Law (Nernst and Planck forms) and explain it with \(S = k_B\ln\Omega\);
  • compute an absolute entropy from heat capacities and latent heats;
  • derive the consequences \(C \to 0\), \(\alpha \to 0\), flat phase boundaries, and the unattainability of 0 K.

9.1  Why worry about absolute zero?

A reversible refrigerator removing heat \(Q_C\) from a cold body at \(T_C\) and dumping it at \(T_H\) needs work (§3.9, §4.4)

\[ W = Q_C\left(\frac{T_H}{T_C} - 1\right) \]

As \(T_C \to 0\) this blows up: extracting even a little heat from something near 0 K costs enormous work. So refrigeration alone cannot reach absolute zero. The Third Law turns this observation into a general principle (§9.4).

9.2  Statements of the Third Law

Nernst statement

For any isothermal process between equilibrium (condensed) states, \(\displaystyle\lim_{T\to0}\Delta S = 0\).

Planck statement

The entropy of a perfect crystal is zero at absolute zero: \(S(T = 0) = 0\).

Nernst says all entropy differences vanish at 0 K, so every state of a substance approaches the same entropy there. Planck sets that common value to zero. Unlike energy, whose zero is a convention, entropy then has an absolute value.

The microscopic reason is \(S = k_B\ln\Omega\) (§4.11). At \(T = 0\) a perfect crystal sits in its single lowest-energy arrangement: \(\Omega = 1\), so \(S = 0\). There is no remaining uncertainty about the microstate.

Residual entropy. If the lowest-energy state is not unique, \(\Omega_0 \gt 1\) and \(S(0) = k_B\ln\Omega_0 \gt 0\). In a crystal of CO, each molecule can point either way (CO or OC) at almost the same energy. If the orientations freeze in at random, \(\Omega_0 = 2^N\), and the crystal keeps \(Nk_B\ln 2\), i.e. \(R\ln 2\) per mole.
How Nernst's statement shows up in chemistry data

For an isothermal reaction, \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\), so \(\Delta S = (\Delta H - \Delta G)/T\). At \(T = 0\), \(\Delta G = \Delta H\), and the limit is \(0/0\). By L'Hôpital's rule, \(\lim\Delta S = (\partial\Delta H/\partial T)_0 - (\partial\Delta G/\partial T)_0\). Measured \(\Delta G(T)\) and \(\Delta H(T)\) curves meet at \(T = 0\) and both become flat there: \(\partial\Delta G/\partial T = -\Delta S \to 0\) and \(\partial\Delta H/\partial T = \Delta C_P \to 0\).

9.3  Absolute entropy

With \(S(0) = 0\) as the reference, the entropy at any temperature is obtained by adding up \(\text{đ}Q/T\) for all the heat needed to bring the substance up from 0 K:

Absolute (Third-Law) entropy

\[ S(T) = \int_0^T\frac{C(T')}{T'}\,dT' + \sum_{\text{transitions}}\frac{\Delta H_{\text{trans}}}{T_{\text{trans}}} \]

The integral uses each phase's own heat capacity; each melting or boiling on the way adds its latent heat divided by its temperature. Tabulated entropies (like the diamond and graphite values in Chapter 7) are made this way. In problems, though, you usually need entropy changes, and the same two pieces (\(\int C\,dT/T\) and \(L/T\)) are what you add up.

Example · An ice cube melting in the kitchen (course example)

A 30 g ice cube at 0 °C melts on a table in a 25 °C kitchen and warms to 25 °C. Find the entropy change of (a) the ice/water, (b) the kitchen, (c) the universe. (\(L = 333\ \text{J/g}\), \(c = 4.186\ \text{J/(g·K)}\).)

Show solution

(a) Melting at 273 K: \(\Delta S_1 = mL/T = (30)(333)/273 = 36.6\ \text{J/K}\). Warming 273 → 298 K: \(\Delta S_2 = mc\ln(T_f/T_i) = (30)(4.186)\ln(298/273) = 11.0\ \text{J/K}\). Total \(47.6\ \text{J/K}\).

(b) The kitchen is a reservoir at 298 K giving up all that heat: \(Q = (30)(333) + (30)(4.186)(25) = 13\,130\ \text{J}\), so \(\Delta S = -13\,130/298 = -44.1\ \text{J/K}\).

(c) \(47.6 - 44.1 = +3.5\ \text{J/K} \gt 0\): irreversible, as heat flowed across a finite temperature difference. The ice gains more entropy than the kitchen loses because it receives the heat at a lower temperature, and \(\Delta S = \int\text{đ}Q/T\).

9.4  Consequences of the Third Law

Each consequence below uses one tool from earlier chapters together with "\(\Delta S \to 0\) as \(T \to 0\)".

(i) Heat capacities vanish

As \(T \to 0\)

\[ C_P \to 0, \qquad C_V \to 0 \]

Reason: \(S(T) = \int_0^T C\,dT'/T'\) must be finite. If \(C\) stayed at a nonzero value, \(\int dT'/T'\) would diverge logarithmically. So the classical ideal gas, with constant \(C_V = \tfrac32Nk_B\), must fail at very low temperature; real matter is quantum there, and its heat capacity falls to zero (for example as \(T^3\) for vibrations in a solid).

(ii) Thermal expansion vanishes

As \(T \to 0\)

\[ \alpha = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} \to 0 \]

Reason: by Nernst, compressing isothermally at \(T \to 0\) changes no entropy, \((\partial S/\partial P)_T \to 0\). The Maxwell relation \((\partial S/\partial P)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P\) (§5.4) then gives \(V\alpha \to 0\). Likewise \((\partial P/\partial T)_V = (\partial S/\partial V)_T \to 0\).

(iii) Phase boundaries become flat

As \(T \to 0\)

\[ \frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v} \to 0 \]

Reason: Clausius–Clapeyron (§7.3) with \(\Delta s \to 0\) and \(\Delta v\) finite. Any coexistence line that reaches 0 K must meet the axis horizontally, as the melting curve of helium does.

(iv) Absolute zero cannot be reached

Cooling is done in alternating steps between two values of a control parameter (volume, pressure, or magnetic field as in adiabatic demagnetisation, §5.6). An isothermal step (in contact with the coldest reservoir available) lowers \(S\) at fixed \(T\); an isentropic step (isolated) then lowers \(T\) at fixed \(S\). The final step can't be isothermal, since that would need a reservoir already at 0 K.

Because \(\Delta S \to 0\), the two curves meet at \(T = 0\). Each vertical drop gets smaller, and so does each move to the left. (In the figure each cycle exactly halves \(T\).) If the curves did not meet, a single isentropic step from a low enough temperature could land exactly on 0 K.

Unattainability statement

Absolute zero cannot be reached by any process in a finite number of steps or in a finite time.
